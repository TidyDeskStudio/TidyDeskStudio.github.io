How Much Interest Could an Extra Loan Payment Save You? How to Work It Out

You've probably wondered: if I paid a bit extra each month, how much sooner would the loan end, and how much interest would I skip? The answer isn't obvious, because interest is worked out on the balance that's left, and every extra payment shrinks that balance for all the months that follow.

The good news is you can see the answer yourself with a small schedule in a spreadsheet. Here's how.

Step 1: Gather four numbers

  • Loan balance you owe today
  • Annual interest rate
  • Regular monthly payment (principal and interest only)
  • Extra amount you're thinking of adding each month

Use the balance from your latest statement, not the original loan amount.

Step 2: Build the monthly schedule

Make a row per month with these columns: month number, opening balance, interest, payment, principal paid, closing balance.

Interest for the month, with the annual rate in cell $B$2 and the opening balance in B6:

=B6*$B$2/12

Principal paid is the payment minus the interest. Closing balance is opening balance minus principal. The next row's opening balance is the previous closing balance.

Keep the payment from overshooting the final month:

=MIN(regular_payment, opening_balance + interest)

Fill down until the closing balance reaches zero. Count the rows: that is the number of months left.

Step 3: Total the interest

Add up the interest column with =SUM(C6:C365). This is the total interest you'd pay if you keep to the normal schedule.

Step 4: Build a second schedule with the extra payment

Copy the sheet and add the extra amount to the payment column:

=MIN(regular_payment+extra, opening_balance + interest)

Now compare the two: months to finish, and total interest. The gap between them is what the extra payment changes.

A worked example

These are invented numbers for illustration only. Say you owe 200,000 at 6 percent a year with a regular payment of 1,199 a month.

  • In month one, interest is 200,000 x 6% / 12 = 1,000. So only 199 of your payment reduces the balance.
  • Add an extra 100 a month and that month pays down 299 instead.
  • Because the balance falls faster, each later month's interest is a little smaller than it would have been, so more of every payment goes to principal.

Run both schedules and you'll see the extra-payment version ends earlier and totals less interest. Your own figures will differ depending on balance, rate and term, which is exactly why it's worth calculating rather than guessing.

Things to check before paying extra

  • Does the lender allow it? Some loans charge a fee for early repayment. Read your terms.
  • How is extra applied? You usually need to say it should go to principal, otherwise it may be treated as a payment in advance.
  • Is the rate fixed or variable? If it changes, update the rate cell and the schedule recalculates.
  • Do you have a cash buffer? Money paid into a loan is not easy to take back out.

Try one-off payments too

Add a column called "one-off extra" so you can enter a lump sum in a particular month, such as a bonus or tax refund. The same closing-balance logic applies. Seeing a single lump sum next to a steady monthly extra can help you decide which suits your cash flow.

Common mistakes

  • Using the original loan amount instead of today's balance.
  • Forgetting escrow, insurance or fees. Keep those out of the principal and interest schedule.
  • Rounding the rate. Use the exact figure from your statement.
  • Not rechecking. Update the balance every few months against your lender's statement.

This is a calculator and record-keeping method, not financial advice. Your lender's statement is the final word on your balance and terms.
