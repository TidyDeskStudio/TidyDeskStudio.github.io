How to Compare Houses You Have Viewed So You Can Actually Decide

After the fourth viewing, the houses start to blur. Was it the one with the big kitchen or the one with the quiet street? Photos on listing sites do not help, because every listing looks good. A short scorecard filled in right after each viewing fixes that, and it takes about five minutes per house.

Step 1: Decide your must-haves before you look

Split your wishes into two lists. Dealbreakers are things the home must have or must not have (a maximum price, minimum bedrooms, a commute limit, step-free access if you need it). Preferences are the things you would like. If a home fails a dealbreaker, stop scoring it. Do this at the kitchen table, before emotions are involved.

Step 2: Pick 8-12 criteria

More than a dozen and you will stop filling it in. A good starting set:

  • Price versus budget
  • Commute or distance to work, school, family
  • Layout and space
  • Kitchen and bathrooms
  • Natural light
  • Condition (roof, windows, damp, heating)
  • Outdoor space or parking
  • Noise and street feel
  • Storage
  • Neighbourhood amenities

Step 3: Weight what matters most

Give each criterion a weight from 1 to 5. A person who works from home might weight light and quiet at 5 and commute at 1. A family might do the opposite. Then score each house from 1 to 5 on every criterion right after you leave.

Step 4: Let a formula do the maths

With weights in column B and one house's scores in column C (rows 2 to 11), the weighted score out of 100 is:

=SUMPRODUCT($B$2:$B$11,C2:C11)/(SUM($B$2:$B$11)*5)*100

Copy the column across for each house and you can see the totals side by side. This works in Excel and Google Sheets.

Worked example (invented numbers)

Say you weight three criteria: price 5, light 3, commute 2 (total weight 10, maximum score 50).

  • House A scores price 4, light 2, commute 5: 20 + 6 + 10 = 36, so 72 out of 100.
  • House B scores price 3, light 5, commute 3: 15 + 15 + 6 = 36, so also 72.

A tie. That is useful information: the scores say the houses are equal on what you chose, so the decision comes down to something the scorecard missed. Add a notes column and write down that missing thing.

Step 5: Record the facts you will forget

Next to the scores, keep a few plain fields for each house: address, asking price, date viewed, agent name, square footage, estimated monthly costs, things to check (boiler age, flood risk, school catchment), and questions to ask. Take a few photos of the things you want to remember, such as the meter cupboard or the view from the back bedroom.

Common mistakes

  • Scoring from memory days later. Fill it in the same day.
  • Changing the weights after you fall for a house. Set them first and leave them.
  • Ignoring running costs. A lower price can hide higher heating, taxes or fees.
  • Letting staging win. Furniture and fresh paint are not part of the home. Score the bones.
  • Treating the total as the answer. It is a tool for a calm conversation, not a verdict. Nothing here is financial or legal advice, so get a proper survey and professional guidance before you buy.

Closing thought
