How to Quote Window Cleaning Jobs Without Underpricing Your Time

Window cleaners usually do not lose money on the work. They lose it on the quote: a price given at the door that ignored the second storey, the conservatory roof or the twenty-minute drive. By the time you notice, the customer is on your regular round at a price that never paid properly.

Here is a way to build a quote you can defend, and a simple record so you can see which jobs earn their place.

Start with your hourly target

Decide what you need to earn per hour of working time, and what you need to cover costs: fuel, water or pole supplies, insurance, tools and your own wages. Write one number. A quote is only good if it clears that figure after travel.

Count what you will actually clean

Make a small price list in a sheet, with a row for each type of item:

  • Standard window (ground floor)
  • Upper-floor window
  • Large patio door or picture window
  • Conservatory roof
  • Frames and sills
  • Skylight or hard-to-reach glass

Give each a time in minutes and a price. Quote = number of each item times its price, added up. For the formula, if counts are in column B and unit price in column C: =SUMPRODUCT(B2:B10,C2:C10).

Add for access and condition

Two houses with ten windows are not the same job. Add a simple surcharge column for:

  • Ladder or extension pole needed
  • Heavy build-up or first clean in a long time
  • Locked gates, steep paths or narrow side access

Keep the extras as a fixed list so you quote the same way each time and do not make it up under pressure.

Do not forget travel

Travel is time you are not paid for unless you price it. Estimate it per job and include a share. If the next customer is two streets away, travel is small. If one job is on the other side of town, either charge for it or ask whether the job fits a route you already run.

A worked example

Example with invented numbers.

  • 12 standard windows at 4 each = 48
  • 3 upper-floor windows at 6 each = 18
  • Conservatory roof = 25
  • Ladder surcharge = 8

Quote = 48 + 18 + 25 + 8 = 99. Estimated work time: 75 minutes. Travel there and back: 20 minutes. Supplies and fuel: 7.

Profit = 99 - 7 = 92 for 95 minutes of time. That is about 58 per hour of total time, before your other overheads. Compare that with your target. If it is well below, the price should go up, or the job should go to a different day when you are already nearby.

Track the real result after the job

After each job, record quoted price, actual time and costs. The quoting method gets better when you compare what you guessed with what happened. Filter by customer or area: some streets look busy and pay badly once travel is counted.

Group jobs into routes

Add an area or postcode column to the sheet, and sort by it. Jobs in the same area can be booked on the same day. Even two or three clustered jobs reduce unpaid driving.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting by the window count only, ignoring access.
  • Leaving travel out of the price completely.
  • Never raising regular prices, even as costs go up.
  • Not recording actual time, so the quote never improves.
  • Taking a one-off job far from your route at a regular price.

Review prices every few months. A small increase announced politely beats resentment about an old price you cannot afford.
