How to Make a 12-Month Cash Flow Forecast for a Small Business (and Spot Tight Months Early)

Plenty of small businesses are profitable over a year and still have a month where the account runs dry. The annual insurance bill lands the same month as a quiet season. A big client pays sixty days late. Tax is due just after you bought new equipment.

A cash flow forecast won't stop those things happening, but it shows you the tight month while it's still months away, when you can do something about it. Here's how to build a simple one, and late in the year is a good time to set up next year's.

This is about planning and keeping records. It isn't financial or tax advice.

1. Start with what's in the bank

A forecast is about cash, not profit. So the starting point is simple: how much money is in your business account on the first day of the forecast? Make a Settings tab and put that in B1, with the first month of the year in B2 (as a date like 1 Jan 2027).

2. List every amount you expect, one row each

Make a Plan tab with these columns:

  • Description - "Client retainer", "Studio rent", "Van insurance"
  • Type - Money in or Money out
  • Category - Sales, Other income, Rent, Wages, Supplies, Software, Insurance, Tax, Loan, Equipment, Marketing, Other
  • Month - the first day of the month you expect it
  • Forecast - the amount you expect
  • Actual - left blank until it happens

Repeat monthly items twelve times, one per month. It feels repetitive, but it lets you change a single month, like a price rise from April or a quieter August, without breaking anything.

Go through last year's bank statements to find the items people forget: yearly renewals, quarterly tax payments, equipment servicing, the holiday month when sales slow down.

3. Total money in and out per month

On a Forecast tab, put the twelve months across row 1 starting with =Settings!$B$2 in B1 and =EDATE(B1,1) in C1, filled right. With Type in column B, Month in D, Forecast in E and Actual in F of the Plan tab, each line's working amount is the actual if you have one, otherwise the forecast. Add a helper column G on the Plan tab: =IF(F2<>"",F2,E2).

Then money in for a month is:

=SUMIFS(Plan!$G:$G,Plan!$B:$B,"Money in",Plan!$D:$D,B$1)

and money out is the same formula with "Money out".

4. Roll the balance forward

Projected closing balance for the first month is opening cash plus in minus out: =Settings!$B$1+B2-B3. For every month after, start from the previous month's closing balance instead: =B4+C2-C3, filled right.

That one row is the whole point of the forecast. You can now see what your account should look like at the end of every month next year.

5. Choose a comfort level and flag low months

Decide the lowest balance you're comfortable with, maybe one month of fixed costs, and put it in B3 on the Settings tab. Then a warning row:

=IF(B4<0,"Overdrawn",IF(B4<Settings!$B$3,"Low cash","OK"))

Colour Low cash amber and Overdrawn red with conditional formatting. Your eye will go straight to them.

6. Update it as each month happens

At the start of each month, open your bank statement and fill the Actual column for last month's rows. Because the helper column swaps in actuals, the balance line now starts from real numbers and the months ahead are recalculated. Add a check column too: =IF(F2="","",IF(ABS(F2-E2)<=E2*0.1,"On track",IF(F2<E2,"Short","Ahead"))) for money in. It shows where your guesses were off so next year's are better.

A worked example (invented numbers)

A two-person print studio starts the year with 6,000 in the bank and a comfort level of 3,000. Sales are fairly steady at around 7,000 a month, with regular costs near 6,900. In March the yearly equipment insurance of 2,000 and a quarterly tax payment of 1,500 fall together, and the forecast balance drops to 2,800, so March shows Low cash. Seeing it in December, the owners move a supplier order to April and chase two invoices early. The new forecast keeps March just above the comfort level.

Common mistakes

  • Forecasting profit instead of cash. An invoice sent in May but paid in July belongs in July.
  • Forgetting yearly and quarterly bills. These are the usual cause of a surprise dip.
  • Being too hopeful about sales. Use what you've actually seen in recent months, not your best month.
  • Building it once and never updating. A forecast is only useful if real numbers replace the guesses.
