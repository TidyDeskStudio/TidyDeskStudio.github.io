How to Chase Late Invoices Without Damaging the Client Relationship

You did the work. You sent the invoice. Then nothing. A week passes, then three, and you can't quite remember whether you already nudged them or whether it's too soon to ask again.

Late payment is rarely about one dramatic refusal to pay. It's usually a pile of small things nobody tracks. Here is a plain method for knowing who owes you what, how late it is, and when to follow up.

Step 1: Put every invoice on one list

One row per invoice, with these columns:

  • Invoice number
  • Client
  • Invoice date
  • Due date
  • Amount
  • Amount paid (so part-payments are handled)
  • Last follow-up date
  • Notes

Keep paid invoices on the list too. They show how each client really behaves over time.

Step 2: Work out the balance and days late

Two formulas do most of the work. Balance still owed:

=E2-F2

Days late, which shows nothing if the invoice isn't due yet or is fully paid:

=IF(OR(G2<=0,D2=""),0,MAX(0,TODAY()-D2))

Here column D is the due date, E the amount, F the paid amount and G the balance. These work the same in Excel and Google Sheets.

Step 3: Sort invoices into aging buckets

Aging simply means grouping unpaid invoices by how overdue they are. A common split is:

  • Not yet due
  • 1 to 30 days late
  • 31 to 60 days late
  • 61 to 90 days late
  • Over 90 days late

A formula to label each row:

=IF(G2<=0,"Paid",IF(H2=0,"Not due",IF(H2<=30,"1-30",IF(H2<=60,"31-60",IF(H2<=90,"61-90","90+")))))

Then total each bucket with =SUMIF(I:I,"31-60",G:G). The totals show at a glance whether the problem is one stubborn invoice or a general slide.

Step 4: Set a follow-up rhythm and stick to it

Decide your steps in advance so you never have to improvise an awkward email. One example:

  1. Day before due: a short friendly reminder with the invoice attached.
  2. Day 3 late: a polite note asking if anything is blocking payment.
  3. Day 14 late: a firmer email stating the amount, the due date and a date you need payment by.
  4. Day 30 late: a phone call or a final written notice, in line with whatever your contract says.

Add a column for the next follow-up date: =J2+7 where J is the last follow-up. Sort by it each Monday and work through the top of the list.

A worked example

These numbers are invented, just to show the layout.

  • Invoice 101, Harbour Studio Example, 400, due 3 Nov, unpaid. 8 days late, bucket 1-30.
  • Invoice 094, Pine & Co Example, 900, due 5 Oct, 300 paid. Balance 600, 37 days late, bucket 31-60.
  • Invoice 088, Larkin Print Example, 250, due 1 Aug, unpaid. 102 days late, bucket 90+.

Total outstanding is 1,250, but 250 of it is the most worrying piece. Without the buckets, that old one hides in the middle of the list. With them, it's the first thing you see.

Keeping the tone friendly

  • Assume a mix-up first. Most late payments are forgotten, misrouted or stuck in someone's approval queue.
  • Be specific: invoice number, amount, due date, how to pay.
  • Reply to every response and update the sheet the same day.
  • Keep a note of what was said. If you ever need to escalate, dates and messages matter.

Common mistakes

  • Waiting until you're annoyed. Early, routine reminders feel normal. Late angry ones don't.
  • Not recording part-payments. You then chase the wrong amount.
  • Vague terms. State the due date on every invoice, not just "net 30" buried in a contract.
  • Forgetting your own list. If the sheet isn't opened weekly, it won't help.

This is a record-keeping method, not legal or financial advice. Rules on late fees and collection differ by place, so check your local rules and your contract.
