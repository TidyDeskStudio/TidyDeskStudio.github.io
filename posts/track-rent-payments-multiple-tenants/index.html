How to Track Rent Payments for Multiple Tenants (Without Missing a Late One)

With one rental, you remember who paid. With three or four, you start checking bank statements at midnight trying to work out whether the tenant in unit 2 paid the full amount or only part of it. A rent log fixes that, and it takes about ten minutes a month once it's set up.

Here's a simple system that works in Excel or Google Sheets, whether you have two units or twenty.

1. Give every rent payment its own row

The most common mistake is one row per tenant with twelve month columns across the page. It looks tidy, but it falls apart the moment someone pays in two parts, pays late, or moves out mid-year. Instead, add one row for each unit for each month. These are the columns you need:

  • Rent month - the month the rent is for (use the 1st, e.g. 1 Oct)
  • Property and Tenant
  • Rent due and Due date
  • Date paid and Amount paid
  • Method - bank transfer, debit order, cash

At the start of each month, add the rows for every unit with the amount due. Then you only fill in the paid columns as money arrives.

2. Let the sheet work out the balance and status

Two formula columns do most of the work. If Rent due is in column D and Amount paid in column G, the balance is:

=IF(D2="","",D2-SUM(G2))

Using SUM(G2) instead of just G2 means a blank payment counts as zero rather than causing an error. A status column then turns that balance into something you can scan:

=IF(D2="","",IF(I2<=0,"Paid",IF(TODAY()>E2,"Late","Due")))

Add conditional formatting so Late shows red and Paid shows green, and your whole month is readable at a glance.

3. Track how late, not just whether it's late

A tenant who is always three days late is a different conversation from one who is three weeks late. A days-late column makes the pattern obvious:

=IF(AND(I2>0,TODAY()>E2),TODAY()-E2,"")

Over a few months you'll see who needs a reminder on the 2nd and who never does.

4. Handle part payments properly

When a tenant pays part of the rent, record exactly what arrived and leave the row open. The balance shows what's still owed, and the status stays Late or Due until it reaches zero. If they pay the rest later, either update the amount paid or add a note. What matters is never marking a part payment as paid.

A quick example

Say you have three units: one at 1,150, one at 1,650 and one at 980, a total of 3,780 a month. In October, the first tenant pays on the 1st by debit order. The second, due on the 3rd, sends 800 on the 9th. The third hasn't paid by the 13th. Your log shows two late rows, a total of 1,830 outstanding, and days late of 10 and 12. That's two messages to send this morning, not a vague feeling that something is off.

5. Put expenses next to the income

Rent collected isn't profit. Keep a second tab for expenses (date, property, category, amount) and add up both per property for the year with SUMIFS. A property that collects the most rent can still be your weakest once repairs and rates are counted, and you'll want those category totals at tax time anyway.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Typing property names slightly differently each time. Use a dropdown so every total adds up.
  • Deleting rows when a tenant leaves. Keep the history; it matters if there's ever a dispute.
  • Only checking the sheet when something feels wrong. Look at it on a set day, like the 5th of each month.

This is about keeping clear records. It isn't tax or legal advice, so check the rules where you live.
