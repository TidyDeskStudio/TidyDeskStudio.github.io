How to Raise Your Prices Next Year Without Guessing (A Margin-First Method)

Your supplier raised their rates again. Your software bill crept up. Your own time is worth more than it was last January. And you still charge what you charged a year ago. Most small business owners know a price rise is due, but they pick a number that feels okay ("maybe 5%?") and hope nobody complains. There is a better way: start from the margin you need, then work backwards.

Step 1: List what each item really costs you

For every product or service you sell, write down the direct cost of delivering one unit. For a product that is materials, packaging and any fees taken per sale. For a service it is the supplies, travel and the paid hours of anyone helping you. Leave your own wages out for now; we will come to those.

Use a simple table with these columns: Item, Current price, Unit cost, Units sold last year.

Step 2: Work out your current margin

Margin is the share of the price you keep after direct costs. The formula is:

=(Price-Cost)/Price

Format the cell as a percentage. Do not confuse this with markup, which divides by cost instead. A item that costs 6 and sells for 10 has a margin of 40% and a markup of about 67%. Mixing the two up is the most common reason people under-price.

Step 3: Decide the margin you need

Look at your yearly overheads: rent, insurance, software, advertising, and a fair wage for yourself. Add them up and divide by your total sales to see what share of each sale they eat. Your target margin must at least cover that share, plus a little profit left over. If overheads take 30% of your sales and you want 10% left as profit, you need a margin of about 40%.

Step 4: Calculate the new price from the target

Rearranging the margin formula gives you the price needed to hit your target:

=Cost/(1-TargetMargin)

Then compare it with your current price and see the gap as a percentage: =(NewPrice-OldPrice)/OldPrice. This tells you how big a rise you actually need, instead of how big a rise feels comfortable.

Step 5: Test what it does to your yearly numbers

Multiply the extra profit per unit by the units you expect to sell. Then ask the uncomfortable question: if some customers leave, how many can you lose before the higher price stops being worth it? A rise that lifts your margin from 30% to 36% can lose a fair number of customers and still leave you with the same profit, but only if you check the numbers instead of assuming.

A worked example (invented numbers)

Say you sell a hand-poured candle gift set. Current price 24, unit cost 15, so margin is (24-15)/24 = 37.5%. Your overheads are about 28% of sales and you want 10% profit, so the target margin is 38%. New price: 15/(1-0.38) = 24.19. That is barely a change, so this item is fine. Now take a second item priced at 12 with a unit cost of 9. Margin: 25%. At a 38% target the price should be 9/0.62 = 14.52, a rise of about 21%. That is too steep to do in one go, so you might round to 13.50 this year and review the packaging cost, which is where most of the 9 is going.

This is the useful part of doing it item by item: you find the one or two products dragging your average down instead of raising everything by the same amount.

How to announce the change

  • Give existing customers a few weeks of notice and say the date the new price starts.
  • Keep the message short and plain: costs went up, here is the new price, thanks for your support.
  • Do not apologise repeatedly. One sentence is enough.
  • Update your listings, quote templates and invoices on the same day.

Common mistakes

  • Using markup instead of margin when setting a target.
  • Forgetting platform and payment fees, which take a slice of every sale.
  • Raising everything by one flat percentage when only some items are the problem.
  • Leaving your own time out of the costs and then wondering why you are busy but not better off.
  • Never revisiting the numbers. Set a calendar reminder to repeat this every year.
