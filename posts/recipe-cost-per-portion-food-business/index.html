How to Work Out the Cost of One Portion When You Cook for Sale

You sell a plate of food for what feels right, the till fills up, and at the end of the month you still can't say whether the chili made money or lost it. The fix is simple but it takes a bit of care: work out what one portion actually costs you, then price from that number instead of from a hunch.

Here is the method, using nothing but a spreadsheet and your supplier receipts.

1. Start with the price you really paid per unit

Write down every ingredient in one recipe. For each one, record what you paid, how much came in the pack, and the unit (grams, ounces, each). Then work out the cost per single unit:

=PackPrice/PackQuantity

Do this from receipts, not from memory. Prices drift, and a ten percent rise on your main protein matters far more than a rise on parsley. Keep a date next to each price so you know how stale it is.

2. Account for what you throw away

This is the step most home-style cost sheets skip. You buy a kilo of onions, but after peeling and trimming you may only use 850 grams. You buy a whole chicken, but the meat you serve is a fraction of the weight on the label.

Add a yield % column: usable weight divided by purchased weight. Then adjust the cost:

=UnitCost/Yield

If a trimmed onion costs you 0.004 per gram bought and the yield is 85%, the usable cost is about 0.0047 per gram. Small on one onion, large across a week of prep.

3. Cost the recipe, then divide by portions

For each ingredient line, multiply the quantity used in the recipe by the adjusted unit cost. Add up the lines. Then divide by how many portions the batch honestly makes:

=SUM(LineCosts)/Portions

Count portions by weighing or measuring a few real servings, not by guessing. If you plate 320 grams of curry but the recipe sheet assumed 250, your cost is wrong from the start.

4. Don't forget the small stuff

Oil, salt, spices, foil, napkins, the container, the lid, the fork. Each is tiny; together they can be a real slice of your cost. Add a packaging line per portion and a flat allowance for pantry basics (for example, a fixed amount per batch that you review every few months).

5. Turn cost into a price

Decide your target food-cost percentage, which is the share of the selling price that goes on ingredients and packaging. Many people aim for somewhere around a third, but it depends on your labour, rent, fuel and fees, so choose a number based on your own overheads. Then:

=CostPerPortion/TargetPercent

Round to a price customers will pay, then check the real percentage the other way: =CostPerPortion/SellingPrice.

A worked example

Invented numbers, for illustration only.

  • Batch of bean chili, 20 portions.
  • Beans, tomatoes, onions, peppers, spices and oil add up to 14.00 after yield adjustments.
  • Rice served alongside: 2.50 for the batch.
  • Bowl, lid and spoon: 0.35 per portion, so 7.00 for 20.

Total batch cost: 14.00 + 2.50 + 7.00 = 23.50. Divide by 20 and each portion costs 1.18. At a target of 35%, the selling price comes out around 3.36, so you might charge 3.75 or 4.00 and the real percentage lands near 30%. If a customer-facing price of 4.00 feels too high for your market, the sheet shows you which ingredient to look at, not just that you are losing money.

Common mistakes

  • Using shelf prices from a different shop. Cost what you buy, where you buy it.
  • Ignoring waste and trim. Yield is where most under-pricing hides.
  • Counting portions by the recipe, not the plate. Measure what you actually serve.
  • Setting the price once and never looking again. Re-check your top five ingredients each month.
  • Mixing units. Grams in one row and ounces in another quietly breaks every total.

Keep it up to date

Put your ingredient list on its own tab, with one row per ingredient, and have each recipe pull its costs from that tab with VLOOKUP. When a price changes you edit one cell and every recipe updates. This works the same in Excel and Google Sheets.
