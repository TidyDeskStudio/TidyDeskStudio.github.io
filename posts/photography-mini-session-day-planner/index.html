Mini-session days are popular because they are short, themed and easy to sell. They are also easy to run badly. Twelve slots fill in a week, three deposits never arrive, one family reschedules twice, and a month later you are editing 200 photos with no clear idea who was promised what by when.

A simple tracker, kept from the first booking to the final gallery, keeps all of that visible. Here is how to set one up.

Step 1: Build the slot list before you advertise

Decide your day first, then create one row per slot. Columns that earn their place:

  • Session date and location.
  • Slot time (for example 10:00, 10:20, 10:40).
  • Status: open, held, booked, completed, cancelled.
  • Client name and contact.
  • Package and price.

Use a dropdown for status so the wording stays consistent. Then you can count open slots at any time with =COUNTIF(D2:D20,"open").

Step 2: Track the deposit, not just the booking

A held slot with no deposit is not really a booking. Add columns for deposit due, deposit paid and balance. The balance is simply:

=G2-H2

Add a date for when the deposit was requested and a flag for slots that have been held more than a few days without payment. You can then release them to the waiting list rather than lose the time silently.

Step 3: Put the gallery promise in a column

This is the part that quietly damages reviews. When you sell a session you usually say something like gallery within two weeks. Write the actual date in the row as soon as the session is booked. A formula keeps it automatic:

=C2+14

Then add a delivered column and a late flag:

=IF(AND(J2="",TODAY()>I2),"LATE","")

Sort by the gallery date each morning and the editing order is decided for you.

Step 4: Note what each family actually needs

A small notes column pays for itself: outfit colours, a nervous toddler, a pet joining in, a request for a specific prop. It takes seconds to write and saves you from asking again on the day.

Step 5: Look at the day as a business afterwards

When the day is done, add up what it brought in and what it cost: location fee, props, assistant, travel and editing time. Then compare:

=SUM(price column)-SUM(cost column)

If you ran two locations, repeat it for each and you will see which was actually worth the setup. Record it in the same workbook so you can compare it with next season.

A worked example

All numbers are invented, for layout only.

  • 12 slots at a session price of 150 each. 10 booked, 2 open.
  • Deposit requested at 50 per booking. 8 paid, 2 outstanding, so 400 received and 100 still to chase.
  • Session date 8 December, so gallery promise is 22 December. Ten galleries are due on the same day.
  • Costs: location 60, props 40, travel 25, assistant 80. Total costs 205 against 1,500 booked, leaving 1,295 before your editing time.

The point of the example is not the figure, it is the two outstanding deposits and the ten galleries due on one date. Both are things you want to see well before they bite.

Common mistakes

  • Taking bookings with no deposit. No-shows then cost you a whole slot.
  • Promising a gallery date and not writing it down. Memory fails when ten sessions land together.
  • Selling too many slots for one editing week. Count the editing hours first.
  • Skipping the cost side. A full day can still make less than it seems.
  • Tracking in messages. Replies get buried, so keep one source of truth.

This is a record-keeping method, not legal, contract or tax advice. Check your own terms and local rules.
