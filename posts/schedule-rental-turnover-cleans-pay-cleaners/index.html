How to Schedule Rental Turnover Cleans and Pay Your Cleaners Correctly

Between one guest leaving at 11 and the next arriving at 4, a rental has to be stripped, cleaned, restocked and checked. If you manage a few properties, or clean them for other people, that window gets squeezed fast. Add a cleaner who asks "how much do I get for last week?" and a host who asks "which units were done on the 14th?", and memory is no longer enough.

This layout covers both the schedule and the pay record in one spreadsheet.

1. A property list

One row per property: name or address, bedrooms, bathrooms, standard clean time, pay per clean, access notes, and special instructions (extra linen, hot tub, laundry location). Put the pay figure here so every job pulls from the same place.

2. A turnover calendar from check-out and check-in dates

List each stay: property, guest check-out date, next check-in date. The cleaning window is the gap between them. Calculate how many days you have:

=C2-B2 where B is check-out and C is next check-in.

Same-day turnovers (a gap of 0) deserve a flag so they are scheduled first:

=IF(C2-B2=0,"Same day","")

Both work in Excel and Google Sheets.

3. Assign cleans, one row per job

The job log columns: date, property, cleaner, start time, finish time, status (Scheduled, Done, Needs recheck), pay amount, paid (Yes/No), paid date, notes. Use a dropdown for cleaner and status to avoid typos that break totals.

4. Use a standard checklist

A short, fixed checklist cuts back-and-forth: strip beds, start laundry, clean bathrooms, kitchen, floors, restock soap and paper, check for damage or left items, photo of the finished bedroom, lock up. Keep it per property if layouts differ. A "Needs recheck" status and a notes cell for damage or missing items give you a paper trail when something is disputed.

5. Pay cleaners from the log

Total what each cleaner is owed for a pay period:

=SUMIFS(Jobs!G:G,Jobs!C:C,A2,Jobs!H:H,"No",Jobs!A:A,">="&StartDate,Jobs!A:A,"<="&EndDate)

Put the period start and end in two cells so you can change them each week. Once paid, change the flag to Yes and fill in the date. If you pay extra for laundry, an add-on column keeps the base pay and the extras visible separately.

Worked example

Invented names and numbers.

  • Lakeside Cabin: guest leaves Saturday 11:00, next guests arrive Saturday 16:00. That is a same-day turnover, flagged.
  • Cleaner: Tomasz Reyes, pay 55 for the property, plus 10 for the extra laundry load.
  • Two other cleans this week at 40 each. Total owed: 55 + 10 + 40 + 40 = 145.
  • The summary shows 145 unpaid until you tick them off.

Common mistakes

  • Booking the cleaner after the guest list changes. When a stay is extended or cancelled, update the calendar first.
  • Paying from memory. A weekly total from the log avoids both overpaying and shorting someone.
  • No damage notes. Record issues the day they are found, with the date and who was there.
  • Ignoring travel time between units. Check the finish time of one job against the start of the next.

This is a record-keeping method, not tax, employment or legal advice. How you classify and pay cleaners depends on local rules, so check them for your area.
