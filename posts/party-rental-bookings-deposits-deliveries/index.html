Party rental businesses live on a handful of weekends. The bounce house, tables, chairs and arch are all in demand on the same Saturday, and one overlooked booking means ringing a customer to say their item is not available. That is hard to recover from.

The fix is a booking sheet that knows which item is out on which date. Here is how to build one.

Step 1: List your items as separate rows

Start with an inventory tab. One row per item, with a code, a name, how many you own, and the rental price. If you own 10 tables, the quantity is 10, not ten separate rows, unless each needs its own tracking.

Step 2: Log each booking with dates and items

On a booking tab, use one row per item per booking. Columns that matter:

  • Booking ID and customer name.
  • Item code and quantity.
  • Delivery date and collection date.
  • Address or delivery area.
  • Status: enquiry, confirmed, delivered, returned.

Splitting by item keeps the clash check simple.

Step 3: Catch clashes with a formula

For single-unit items, a clash means two confirmed bookings overlap. The test for a row is whether another row has the same item with overlapping dates. In plain terms, another booking overlaps if it starts before this one ends, and ends after this one starts:

=COUNTIFS(B:B,B2,C:C,"<="&D2,D:D,">="&C2)

Here B is the item code, C is the delivery date and D is the collection date. The result counts every booking of that item whose dates overlap, including this one, so anything above 1 is a clash. Wrap it in =IF(...>1,"CLASH","") for a clear flag, and add conditional formatting to turn it red. For items you own several of, compare the count against the quantity instead.

Step 4: Treat the deposit as a separate status

Add three columns: deposit due, deposit paid and balance. The balance is =F2-G2. Then add a flag for bookings where the delivery date is within a week and the balance is still unpaid. Those are the ones to ring on Monday, not on delivery day.

Step 5: Log damage and repairs against the item

When something comes back with a tear, a stain or a missing part, note it against the item and date, with a cost to repair or replace. Over a season you will see which items repeatedly need work and whether a particular item earns what it costs to keep.

A worked example

All names and numbers are invented, for layout only.

  • Item BH-1, a bounce house, price 180 per day. One unit owned.
  • Booking A: delivery 14 Dec, collection 15 Dec.
  • Booking B: delivery 15 Dec, collection 16 Dec.
  • The formula counts 2 overlapping bookings for BH-1 on 15 Dec, so both rows show CLASH. Depending on your own turnaround, you either move one booking or schedule a same-day swap on purpose.
  • Revenue for the month for that item: 180 x 9 days booked = 1,620. Repairs: 60. Net: 1,560.

Common mistakes

  • Tracking bookings in a calendar only. A calendar does not know how many of an item you own.
  • Counting an enquiry as a booking. Only confirmed rows should block the item.
  • Ignoring turnaround time. Cleaning and checking take time, so build it into the collection date.
  • Taking balances on the doorstep with no record. Log every payment.
  • Not noting damage straight away. You forget by the next booking.

This is a record-keeping method, not legal, insurance or tax advice. Check your own contracts and local rules.
