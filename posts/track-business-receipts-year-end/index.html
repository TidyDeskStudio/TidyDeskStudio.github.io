How to Keep Track of Business Expenses and Receipts So Year-End Isn't a Scramble

Ask anyone who runs a small business what they dread about year-end and it's rarely the sums. It's the card charge from March that could be anything, the receipt that was in a coat pocket, and an evening spent scrolling through emails trying to match them up.

The cure is boring and it works: log each expense when it happens, note where its receipt lives, and check the gaps once a week. With two months of the year left, now is a good time to start, and to catch up on what's already gone by.

This post is about keeping tidy records. It isn't tax or accounting advice. What counts as a business expense and how long to keep receipts depends on where you live, so check the rules locally or ask an accountant.

1. Set up one row per expense

Make an expense log with these columns:

  • Date - the date on the receipt
  • Vendor - who you paid
  • Category - a dropdown such as Supplies, Software, Travel, Vehicle, Phone & internet, Marketing, Rent, Equipment, Professional fees, Other
  • Paid with - business card, business account, personal card, cash
  • Amount - the total you paid
  • Tax included - any sales tax or VAT shown on the receipt
  • Receipt ref - where the receipt is: a folder name, a file name or "email 14 Oct"
  • Notes - what it was for, in plain words

Keep categories few and fixed. If you invent a new one every week, the totals stop meaning anything.

2. Make missing receipts impossible to ignore

Add a Receipt status column. Pick how many days you allow to file a receipt, say 14, and put it in $L$1. With date in A and receipt ref in G:

=IF(G2<>"","Filed",IF(TODAY()-A2>$L$1,"Receipt missing","Get receipt"))

Add conditional formatting to turn "Receipt missing" red. A count at the top keeps you honest: =COUNTIF(I:I,"Receipt missing").

3. Separate tax and track personal spending

If receipts show sales tax or VAT, a Net column is simply =E2-F2. Whether you can do anything with that tax depends on your situation, but having it recorded saves a lot of digging later.

When you pay for a business cost with a personal card, mark it and add a Paid back on date column. A status like =IF(AND(D2="Personal card",J2=""),"Reimburse me","") shows what the business still owes you.

4. See where the money goes each month

On a summary tab, list your categories down column A and months across row 1 as first-of-month dates. Then:

=SUMIFS(Log!$E:$E,Log!$C:$C,$A2,Log!$A:$A,">="&B$1,Log!$A:$A,"<"&EDATE(B$1,1))

Add a monthly budget per category next to it and you can spot drift early: software creeping up, or travel that doubled in one month.

5. Build a five-minute weekly habit

  1. Photograph or save every receipt from the week into one folder.
  2. Open your card and bank apps and add any rows you missed.
  3. Fill the Receipt ref for each new row.
  4. Chase anything still showing Get receipt: most vendors can email a copy.

Name receipt files with the date and vendor, like 2026-10-14 printer ink.pdf, so they sort themselves.

A worked example (invented numbers)

A one-person design studio logs 23 expenses in October totalling 1,860. Software is 240 against a budget of 200, so it shows Over budget, and a forgotten trial turns up in the list. Two rows show Get receipt and one shows Receipt missing: a train ticket from 28 days ago. The owner finds it in an old email, adds the reference and the count drops to zero. A 65 printer cartridge bought on a personal card shows Reimburse me until the transfer is made.

Common mistakes

  • Logging once a year from bank statements. You'll have amounts but not the story behind them.
  • Too many categories. Ten or so is plenty for most small businesses.
  • Mixing personal and business without marking it.
  • Keeping receipts with no reference in the log, so finding one still means a search.
