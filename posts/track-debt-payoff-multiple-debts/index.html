How to Track Paying Off Several Debts at Once (and Actually See Progress)

When you owe money in four or five places, it's hard to tell whether you're getting anywhere. A card statement here, a loan app there, a medical bill on paper. You make the payments, but the total never seems to move, and that's when people lose heart.

A simple tracker changes that. Put every debt on one page, log every payment, and let a few formulas show what's left and how far you've come. Seeing a balance shrink month by month makes it much easier to keep going.

This post is about keeping clear records of what you owe and pay. It isn't financial advice. For decisions about consolidating, refinancing or which debt to pay first, talk to a qualified adviser or a free debt charity in your country.

1. List every debt in one place

Gather your latest statements and make one row per debt with these columns:

  • Debt - a name you'll recognise, like "Blue card" or "Car loan"
  • Type - credit card, car loan, student loan, personal loan, store card, medical, family or friend
  • Starting balance - what you owed on the day you started tracking
  • APR - the yearly interest rate, from the statement
  • Min payment - the minimum you must pay each month

Include the awkward ones too, like money owed to a relative. If it's on your mind, it belongs on the list.

2. Log payments on a separate tab

Make a Payments tab with Date, Debt (a dropdown that pulls from your list of debt names), Amount and Kind (Regular or Extra). Mark anything above the minimum as Extra. Later you'll be able to see how much those extra payments added up to over the year.

One row per payment, entered the day you pay. It takes seconds and means you never have to reconstruct the month from bank statements.

3. Add the progress formulas

Back on the Debts tab, add columns that work things out from the payment log. With debt names in A and the payments in a tab called Payments (debt in column B, amount in C):

  • Paid so far: =SUMIFS(Payments!C:C,Payments!B:B,A2)
  • Balance left: =MAX(0,C2-F2) (starting balance minus paid so far)
  • % paid off: =IF(C2=0,"",MIN(1,F2/C2))

Keep in mind this balance is a tracker, not your lender's figure. Interest keeps getting added, so check your statements now and then and update the starting balance if the gap grows.

4. Estimate how many months are left

A built-in function called NPER estimates how many payments it takes to clear a balance at a given rate. With APR in D, minimum payment in E and balance left in G:

=IFERROR(ROUNDUP(NPER(D2/12,-E2,G2),0),"Never")

If it says "Never", the minimum payment doesn't cover the monthly interest, and the balance won't go down at that payment. Spotting that early is useful in itself.

5. Decide where extra money goes

Two common ways people order their debts:

  • Smallest balance first (often called the snowball). Clearing a small debt quickly gives you a win and frees up its payment for the next one.
  • Highest interest first (often called the avalanche). Usually means less interest overall, but the first win can take longer.

Either way, keep paying the minimum on everything and point any extra at one debt at a time. Your tracker shows which debt that is and how fast it's moving. Which order suits you is a personal choice. If you're unsure, ask an adviser.

A worked example (invented numbers)

Say you have three debts. A store card of 400 at 24.9% with a 25 minimum, a credit card of 4,200 at 21.9% with a 150 minimum, and a car loan of 11,800 at 6.9% with a 320 minimum. In February a tax refund lets you pay 400 extra on the store card, and it shows as Cleared. Its 25 a month now goes on top of the credit card's 150. After ten months the card shows 1,950 paid, so the tracker reads a balance left of 2,250 and 46% paid off. Your statement will show a bit more because of interest, so you update the figure each quarter.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Leaving a debt off the list because it's embarrassing. The plan only works if it's complete.
  • Skipping the log and relying on memory. Enter each payment when you make it.
  • Ignoring interest. Compare with your statements regularly.
  • Only looking at the total. Look at each debt too. One cleared debt is real progress, even if the total still looks big.
