How to Price Weekly Meal Prep for a Client (So Groceries Don't Eat Your Pay)

You can cook a week of meals in your sleep. Putting a price on that week is harder. Charge too little and the groceries, the drive and the cleanup quietly swallow your pay. Charge too much with no explanation and clients hesitate.

Here is a method that starts from what a week really costs you, so the price you quote has something solid behind it.

Step 1: List every cost of one client-week

For one typical client, write down each cost for a single week:

  • Groceries - from receipts, not from memory.
  • Shopping time - hours in the store and driving there.
  • Cooking time - hours at the client's kitchen or your own.
  • Packaging - containers, labels, foil.
  • Travel - distance to the client and back.
  • Cleanup - time and supplies.

Time is the one most often left out. If it takes six hours in total and you only count the cooking, you are working for free for the rest.

Step 2: Decide your hourly rate

Pick the hourly rate you want to earn for your time, after your own costs such as insurance and equipment. Then multiply by all the hours from Step 1, not just the time at the stove.

=Hours*Rate

Step 3: Add it up

Weekly price before any margin:

=Groceries+Packaging+Travel+(Hours*Rate)

Then add a small buffer for things that go up, such as ingredient prices or an extra dish. Many people use a percentage, say 5 to 10 percent, and adjust it from their own experience.

A worked example

Invented numbers, just to show the layout. A client wants five dinners for two.

  • Groceries: 85
  • Packaging: 8
  • Travel: 6
  • Hours: shopping 1.5, cooking 4, cleanup 0.5 = 6 hours
  • Rate: 25 per hour, so time = 150

Total before buffer: 85 + 8 + 6 + 150 = 249. With a 5 percent buffer, about 261. Now you can quote a clear weekly price and explain what it covers.

Step 4: Check it against what really happened

After each week, enter the real grocery receipt and the real hours. A simple check:

=Charged-Groceries-Packaging-Travel

Divide the result by the actual hours worked and you have what you really earned per hour for that client. If it is lower than the rate you wanted, the quote needs to change, or the menu does.

Step 5: Spot the clients who cost more than they pay

Keep one row per client per week: client, date, groceries, hours, amount charged. Sort by earnings per hour. Clients at the bottom are usually the ones with expensive ingredients, long drives or menus that change every week. Options include a minimum order, a grocery cap, or a menu with fewer premium items.

Common mistakes

  • Pricing from groceries alone. Your time is the bigger cost.
  • Estimating groceries from memory. Use receipts.
  • Never reviewing a quote. Prices change; so should your numbers.
  • Treating every client the same. Distance, kitchen set-up and menu style all matter.

This is a record-keeping and planning method, not financial or tax advice.
