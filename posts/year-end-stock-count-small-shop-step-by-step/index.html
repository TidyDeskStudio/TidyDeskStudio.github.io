How to Do a Year-End Stock Count for a Small Shop

Every shop's records say one thing and the shelves say another. A box was opened and never logged, a return went back to the wrong bin, something walked out the door. A year-end stock count is how you find the gap and put a value on what you are holding. Done badly it eats a weekend and still leaves you unsure. Done in an orderly way it takes a few hours.

Step 1: Prepare before count day

Decide the cut-off. Pick a date and time, and stop receiving or selling stock during the count if you can, or note down everything that moves while you count. Print or open a count sheet with a row per item. Tidy the shelves first so each product sits in one place only. Most miscounts come from the same item living in two spots.

Step 2: Set up the count sheet

Use these columns:

  • SKU or item name
  • Location
  • System quantity (what your records say)
  • Counted quantity
  • Difference
  • Unit cost
  • Value
  • Notes (damaged, expired, returned)

Keep the system quantity out of sight of the person counting if you can. Seeing the expected number tempts people to round toward it.

Step 3: Count in a pattern

Work shelf by shelf, left to right, top to bottom, and mark each shelf as done. Count in groups of five or ten rather than one by one. For anything with hundreds of units, such as screws or beads, weigh a known sample and calculate the rest. If two people are available, have the second person recount only the items where the first count differs from your records.

Step 4: Calculate differences and values

The difference is simple:

=D2-C2

where D is counted and C is the system quantity. A negative number means you hold less than you thought. The value of what you hold is:

=D2*F2

Add the value column with =SUM(G2:G200). This gives you the total stock value at cost. Use what you paid, not the selling price.

Step 5: Investigate the big differences

Sort the sheet by the absolute value of the difference and look at the top ten. Common causes: a delivery counted into stock twice, a bundle sold but only one item deducted, samples given away, or a data entry typo. Recount the large ones before you adjust anything.

Step 6: Write the adjustments down

Update your records to match the count and keep the count sheet itself, dated. If you have an accountant or file your own tax return, they will want the total stock value and the date of the count. This guide is about record keeping, not tax advice, so check your local rules on how stock is valued and reported.

A worked example (invented numbers)

A small candle shop counts 3 items. Soy wax: records say 40 kg, counted 36, unit cost 5, so the difference is -4 and the value is 180. Glass jars: records 200, counted 210, unit cost 1.20, difference +10 and value 252. Wicks: records 500, counted 500, unit cost 0.10, value 50. Total value: 482. The jar surplus suggests a delivery was entered at the wrong quantity, and the wax shortfall is probably spillage during pouring, which you may now start logging as waste.

Common mistakes

  • Counting while sales and deliveries are still moving stock.
  • Valuing stock at the selling price instead of cost.
  • Forgetting stock stored at home, in the car or at a market stall.
  • Not separating damaged or expired goods.
  • Adjusting the records without recording why.
