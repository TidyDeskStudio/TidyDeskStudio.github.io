How to Keep Track of Insurance Policies and Renewal Dates (Before an Auto-Renewal Surprises You)

Most households have somewhere between four and ten insurance policies: home or renters, car, health, life, maybe pet, travel, phone, or a specific item. Each renews on a different day, with a different company, often with a different login. Then one arrives by email or post with a higher premium and you have a few days to decide whether to keep it.

A single list of all your policies turns that into a calm, planned decision. Here's how to build it.

Start with a full inventory

Gather every policy document and every email that says "your policy". Look in your inbox for the words renewal, policy number and certificate. Check your bank statements for regular payments to insurers, since that catches the ones you forgot. Then give each policy a row.

Columns that earn their place

  • Type - home, car, health, life, pet, travel, other.
  • Provider - the company name.
  • Policy number - so you can find it in a hurry.
  • What it covers - one short line: "Car A, comprehensive".
  • Start date and Renewal date.
  • Premium and How often paid - monthly, yearly.
  • Annual cost - calculated, so monthly and yearly policies compare fairly.
  • Excess or deductible - what you pay on a claim.
  • Cover limit - the main maximum payout.
  • Auto-renews? - Yes or No.
  • Contact - phone or claims line, not a password.
  • Where the documents are - folder name or drawer.

Do not store passwords or full account numbers in the sheet. A pointer to where the paperwork lives is enough.

The formulas

Annual cost, with premium in F and frequency in G ("Monthly" or "Yearly"):

=IF(G2="Monthly",F2*12,F2)

Days until renewal, with the renewal date in E:

=E2-TODAY()

A reminder flag that turns on 45 days before renewal:

=IF(AND(E2-TODAY()<=45,E2-TODAY()>=0),"Review now","")

Forty-five days is a good lead time. It's long enough to ask for quotes, short enough that the numbers are still current.

A worked example

This is invented data to show the layout.

  • Car policy, renews Mar 14, pays monthly, auto-renews: Yes.
  • Renters policy, renews Aug 2, pays yearly, auto-renews: Yes.
  • Pet policy, renews Nov 28, pays monthly, auto-renews: Yes.

On Oct 14, the pet policy shows "Review now". You have time to read the renewal letter, compare it with last year's premium, and decide to keep, change or shop around, instead of finding the new price on your statement.

What to do when the flag appears

  1. Open the renewal notice and compare the new premium to your Annual cost column.
  2. Check that the cover still matches your life: new car, new address, bigger purchases, someone added or removed.
  3. Look at the excess or deductible. A cheaper premium sometimes just means you pay more when something happens.
  4. Ask the current provider whether they can do better, and get one or two other quotes if you want to compare.
  5. Record the decision and the new date in the sheet.

Add a total at the top

Sum the Annual cost column and put it in a cell at the top of the page. Many people have never seen this number. It makes it easy to ask whether you are insured for the right things, and not paying twice for the same cover (for example, a phone covered by both a bank account and a separate plan).

Common mistakes

  • Tracking only the policy end date. Some policies need action before it, such as notice periods.
  • Forgetting auto-renew. Mark which ones renew themselves, those need you to act first.
  • Ignoring small policies. A forgotten pet or gadget plan still bills every month.
  • Not updating after changes. If you move or buy something, update the row.
  • Keeping the sheet separate from the paperwork. Write down where the documents are.

This is a record-keeping tool, not insurance or financial advice. Check your own policy wording and local rules for what applies to you.
