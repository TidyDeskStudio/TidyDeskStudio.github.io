How to Keep Track of Warranties and Receipts So You Can Actually Find Them When Something Breaks

The dryer stops heating eight months after you bought it. You know there was a warranty. You do not know how long it lasted, where the receipt went, or whether you registered it. So you spend an evening digging through email and a kitchen drawer, and you still are not sure.

The fix is boring and takes about two minutes per purchase: write each big purchase down once, in one place, with the dates that matter. Here is how to set that up.

Decide what counts as a big purchase

You do not need to log a toaster. Pick a threshold you can remember, such as anything over 100, plus anything that comes with a warranty card regardless of price. Appliances, electronics, furniture, tools, a mattress, a heat pump, a bike. If you would be annoyed to replace it, log it.

The columns that matter

Open a spreadsheet and make one row per item with these columns:

  • Purchase date
  • Item (plain words: Front-load washer)
  • Store
  • Price
  • Model and serial number (take a phone photo of the label the day it arrives; serials are painful to find behind a heavy machine later)
  • Warranty length in months
  • Return window in days
  • Where the receipt is (filing box, email folder, phone photo, or Not found)

Storing the warranty length rather than the end date is the trick. You copy the number from the paperwork and let formulas do the date maths.

Let formulas work out the deadlines

With the purchase date in column A, the warranty months in column F and the return days in column G, these formulas work in Excel and Google Sheets:

  • Warranty end date: =EDATE(A2,F2)
  • Days left on warranty: =H2-TODAY()
  • Return deadline: =A2+G2

Then add one note column that tells you what to do: =IF(I2<0,"Expired",IF(I2<=90,"Check before it ends","Covered")). Change 90 to however much notice you like. Sort by days left and the items that need attention rise to the top.

Add a simple claims list

Make a second tab for claims: date reported, item, what went wrong, who you contacted, status (Open, Repaired, Replaced, Denied) and what it covered. When the same fridge fails twice you will want the history in one place, and it helps if you ever decide a model is not worth buying again.

A worked example

These numbers are invented. You buy a washer on 3 March for 780 with a 24-month warranty and a 30-day return window.

  • Warranty end date: 3 March, two years later
  • Return deadline: 2 April
  • Receipt kept: Email folder

In month 22 your sheet shows about 60 days left, so you run a full cycle, check the door seal and the drain pump, and report anything odd while the manufacturer still has to listen. Without the row, that date passes unnoticed.

Fix the receipt problem at the source

  • Photograph paper receipts before you leave the shop and file the photo in a folder named for the year.
  • Forward emailed receipts to a folder rather than leaving them in the main inbox.
  • If a receipt is truly missing, mark it Not found. A visible gap reminds you to ask the store for a copy or check your card statement, which often shows the date and the amount.

Common mistakes

  • Logging only the purchase price. The dates are the valuable part.
  • Assuming the warranty starts on the delivery date. Read the paperwork. Some start at purchase, some at delivery, some at registration.
  • Forgetting the return window. It is much shorter than the warranty and easy to miss.
  • Never reviewing the list. Spend ten minutes at the start of each quarter sorting by days left.

This is a record-keeping method, not legal advice. Always check the seller's or manufacturer's own terms, since coverage and consumer rights differ by country and product.
