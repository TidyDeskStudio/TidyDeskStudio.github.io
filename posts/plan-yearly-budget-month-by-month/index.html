How to Plan a Yearly Budget Month by Month (So the Expensive Months Don't Catch You Out)

Most budgets are monthly: one number for groceries, one for fun, one for bills, the same every month. Then December arrives with gifts and heating, or August brings school shoes and a trip, and the budget breaks. After two broken months it's easy to give up on it altogether.

A yearly budget planned month by month fixes that. You still budget each month, but you decide in advance that some months cost more. Here's how to set one up for next year, starting with a quick look back at this one.

This is a planning and record-keeping method, not financial advice.

1. Look back at last year first

Before planning, find out where the money actually went. Download your bank and card statements for the last twelve months, or as many as you can, and total them into roughly ten to fifteen categories:

  • Money in: Pay, Side income, Other
  • Bills: Housing, Utilities, Phone & internet, Insurance, Subscriptions
  • Living: Groceries, Transport, Eating out, Kids, Health, Personal
  • Occasional: Gifts, Holidays, Home & repairs, Car costs
  • Saving: Savings, Extra debt payments

Put last year's total beside each category. Don't judge it yet. The aim is a realistic starting point, not a confession.

2. Build a 12-month plan grid

On a Plan tab, list categories down column A, leave B for last year's total, and put month numbers 1 to 12 across C1:N1. Fill each cell with what you expect that category to cost in that month.

This is where the yearly view earns its keep. Gifts might be small most months and large in December. Utilities rise in winter. Car insurance might be one payment in May. Add a total in column O with =SUM(C2:N2) and a change column with =O2-B2 so you can see where next year differs from last year, and decide if that's on purpose.

3. Check money in covers money out each month

Under the grid, total income and spending for each month. A simple monthly left-over row is =C3-C20 if income is in row 3 and total spending is in row 20. If a month goes negative, you have options while it's still a plan: move a cost, save towards it in the months before, or trim another category.

4. Log what really happens

Make a Log tab with Date, Description, Category and Amount. Use a dropdown for Category pointing at your plan's category list, so names always match. Logging once a week from your bank app takes a few minutes.

5. Compare plan to date with actual to date

Comparing a whole year's plan with three months of spending isn't useful. Compare like with like. Plan to date for a category, counting the months so far:

=SUMIF($C$1:$N$1,"<="&MONTH(TODAY()),C2:N2)

Actual to date from the log, for this year only:

=SUMIFS(Log!$D:$D,Log!$C:$C,$A2,Log!$A:$A,">="&DATE(2027,1,1),Log!$A:$A,"<="&TODAY())

Then a status: =IF(Q2=0,"Nothing yet",IF(Q2>P2*1.05,"Over plan","On track")), where P is plan to date and Q is actual to date. For income rows, flip it so falling short shows Behind.

6. Give each goal a monthly number

Goals like "save for a holiday" or "pay off the card" stick better with a number per month. On a Goals tab list Goal, Target, Saved so far and Deadline. Amount needed per month:

=MAX(0,(B2-C2)/MAX(1,DATEDIF(TODAY(),D2,"m")))

Progress is =C2/B2 formatted as a percentage. Put the monthly amount for each goal into your plan grid as a Saving line, so goals are part of the budget, not an afterthought.

A worked example (invented numbers)

A household finds it spent 1,900 on gifts last year, almost all in November and December. For next year they plan 50 a month from January to October and 700 in each of November and December, the same 1,900 total but with the big months expected. Utilities are planned at 220 in winter months and 130 in summer. By the end of March, groceries show 1,560 actual against 1,500 planned, which is On track within 5 percent, while eating out is Over plan. Their holiday goal of 2,400 by August, with 600 saved, needs 360 a month.

Common mistakes

  • Planning every month the same. Real life has lumpy months. Plan for them.
  • Skipping the look back. A plan built on guesses usually guesses low.
  • Too many categories. If you need a minute to decide where a coffee goes, merge some.
  • Checking against the full year. Always compare plan to date with actual to date.
  • Leaving goals off the plan. Money for a goal needs its own line or it gets spent.
