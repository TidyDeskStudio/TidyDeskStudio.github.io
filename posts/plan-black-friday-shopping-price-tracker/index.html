How to Plan Black Friday Shopping So You Only Buy What Was Already on Your List

Every year the same thing happens. You open a sale page for one item, and an hour later you have six tabs open, a cart full of things you did not plan to buy, and no idea whether the "40% off" tag means anything. The fix is boring but it works: decide what you need and what you will pay before the sales start, then let the sales come to you.

Here is a simple way to do that with a spreadsheet, using nothing more than a few columns.

Step 1: Set one number for the whole event

Pick a total you can spend across Black Friday and the days after it. Not per store, not per item: one total. Write it at the top of your sheet. Everything else in the plan has to fit under it, which forces the useful trade-offs early.

If you already have a holiday gift budget, take a slice of it for the sales instead of adding a second pot of money. Otherwise the sales spending quietly becomes extra.

Step 2: Write the wish list before you look at any sale

Open a sheet and make these columns:

  • Item - specific enough to search for ("kettle, 1.7 litre", not "kitchen stuff")
  • For - who it is for, or "me"
  • Priority - 1 = must have, 2 = nice, 3 = only if it is a great price
  • Normal price - what it costs on an ordinary week
  • Target price - the most you will pay
  • Store
  • Bought? - Yes or No
  • Price paid

The order matters. If you write the list first, a sale can only help you buy something you already wanted. If you browse first, the sale decides what you want.

Step 3: Record the normal price now

This is the step most people skip, and it is the one that catches fake discounts. A "was" price on a sale tag is whatever the store chooses to print. Your own record of what the item cost in October is more reliable.

Check each wish-list item this week and type the price in. Do it again a few days before the sales. If the price crept up just before the event, you will see it in your own numbers.

Step 4: Work out the real saving

Add two formula columns. If normal price is in column D, price paid in column H:

  • Saving in money: =D2-H2
  • Saving as a percentage: =(D2-H2)/D2 formatted as a percent

To see whether you are inside budget, put your price-paid column total in one cell with =SUM(H2:H40) and your event limit in another, then subtract: =B1-SUM(H2:H40). A negative number means stop.

A worked example

Example with invented numbers. Your event budget is 300. Your list has four items:

  • Kettle: normal 45, target 32, priority 2
  • Headphones for a friend: normal 80, target 60, priority 1
  • Board game: normal 36, target 25, priority 2
  • Coat for yourself: normal 150, target 110, priority 1

Add the targets: 32 + 60 + 25 + 110 = 227. That leaves 73 of room. A sale price at or below target gets a Yes. A sale price above target gets skipped, even if it is "30% off", because 30% off a number the store raised is not a saving.

Step 5: Watch for the two common traps

  • Bundles you did not want. A "buy three, save more" offer only counts if you wanted three.
  • Shipping and returns. Add delivery to the price paid, otherwise your sheet says you won when you did not.

Common mistakes

  • Setting a budget but not tracking what you actually pay, so the total drifts.
  • Putting "other" as a priority-1 line. If it is not specific, it is not a plan.
  • Buying a priority-3 item because it is cheap. Cheap and needed are different things.
  • Forgetting to mark items Bought, then buying them twice in the second sale.

Keep the sheet open during the sales and update it as you buy. It takes a few seconds per purchase and saves you from the January surprise on your statement.
