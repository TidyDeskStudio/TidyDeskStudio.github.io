How to Compare Contractor Bids and Keep a Renovation on Budget

You have three quotes for the same kitchen. One is much lower, one is much higher, and each is laid out differently. Which one is actually cheapest? You cannot tell until the quotes sit in the same format. A short comparison table does that, and a budget sheet then keeps the project honest once work begins.

Step 1: Break the job into the same line items

Before you ask for bids, list the scope in parts: demolition, plumbing, electrical, cabinets, flooring, painting, cleanup and disposal, permits. Give every contractor the same list. When the answers come back, you compare like for like instead of one total against another.

Step 2: Build the comparison table

Put your line items down the left side and one column per contractor. Under each, enter the quoted amount for that item. Where a contractor left an item out, write Not included, not zero. A zero looks like a bargain and is not.

Add these rows at the bottom:

  • Quoted total: =SUM(B2:B12)
  • Missing items: =COUNTIF(B2:B12,"Not included")
  • Materials allowance: who supplies them, and how much is assumed
  • Start date and duration
  • Payment schedule: deposit, stages, final
  • Warranty on the work

The lowest total with the most missing items is rarely the lowest real cost.

Step 3: Ask the questions the quote does not answer

  • Does the price include permits and cleanup?
  • What happens if you find rot or old wiring behind a wall?
  • How are changes priced, and are they agreed in writing?
  • Who is on site day to day?

Write the answers in the table next to the quote. Memory is unreliable three weeks later.

Step 4: Turn the chosen bid into a budget

Once you pick a contractor, copy their line items into a budget sheet with the columns Item, Budgeted, Spent and Remaining. Remaining is =B2-C2. Add a Contingency row at the top. A common habit is to set aside 10 to 15 percent of the total, because renovations tend to uncover surprises. That is only a rule of thumb, so adjust it to the age and condition of your home.

A worked example

All numbers are invented.

  • Contractor A: total 14,200, two items not included
  • Contractor B: total 16,800, nothing missing
  • Contractor C: total 15,500, one item not included

Say the missing items in A would cost about 1,900 to add, and the one in C about 600. Then A's real figure is near 16,100 and C's near 16,100 too, while B stays at 16,800. The spread is much narrower than the headline suggested, and now your decision can rest on schedule, references and warranty instead of a gap that was mostly omissions.

Step 5: Log every payment and change

Keep a short log: date, what was paid, which line item it belongs to, and whether it was in the original quote. Anything outside the quote is a change order. Record its amount and who approved it. At the end you can see exactly where the budget moved.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing totals only. Compare scope first.
  • Paying too much up front. Tie payments to finished stages where you can.
  • Verbal changes. If it is not written down, it will be disputed.
  • No contingency. One surprise should not sink the plan.
  • Forgetting your own costs: storage, eating out, a temporary kitchen.

This is a record-keeping approach, not legal or financial advice. Check local rules on permits and contracts.
