How to Make a Bill Due-Date Calendar (and Catch Autopay Mistakes Before They Cost You)

Autopay feels like a way to stop thinking about bills. Then the card expires, the amount jumps, or three payments land the day before payday and the account runs dry. The fix is not to ditch autopay. It is to keep one simple calendar that shows every bill, when it leaves your account, and whether you have actually checked it lately.

Step 1: Gather every recurring bill

Go through the last two months of bank and card statements and write down everything that repeats: rent or mortgage, utilities, phone, internet, insurance, loan repayments, memberships, and any subscription. Include the small ones. They are the ones that drift unnoticed.

Step 2: Set up the columns

One row per bill, with these columns:

  • Bill name
  • Amount (and a note if it varies)
  • Due day (a number from 1 to 31)
  • Paid from (which account or card)
  • Autopay? (Yes or No)
  • Last checked (a date)
  • Notes (login reminder, account number ending, who to call)

Never put full account numbers or passwords in the sheet. The last four digits are plenty.

Step 3: Turn due days into a calendar view

Make a second section with the days 1 to 31 down the side. Next to each day, show the total due on that day with a sum that looks at your bill list:

=SUMIF(C2:C40,A2,B2:B40)

Here column C holds the due day, column A in the calendar holds the day number, and column B holds the amount. Now you can see at a glance that the 1st has a heavy load and the 20th is empty. That is information you can act on.

Step 4: Check the autopay column properly

This is the part most people skip. Each month, tick through these questions for every autopay bill:

  1. Is the payment method still valid (not expired, not replaced)?
  2. Did the amount match what you expected?
  3. Will the account have enough in it on the due day?
  4. Is the bill still needed?

Record the date you checked in Last checked. Then add a conditional highlight that flags anything not checked for more than 60 days. A formula such as =TODAY()-F2>60 in a conditional-format rule does the job in Excel and Google Sheets.

Step 5: Add a cash cushion test

Add a cell for the balance you expect in the paying account at the start of each week. Subtract the week's due amounts from it. If the result goes negative, you know before the bank tells you. Moving a due date with the provider (many allow it for free) is often easier than it sounds.

A worked example (invented numbers)

Suppose five bills leave on the 1st: rent 900, internet 45, phone 38, insurance 62 and a gym membership 25, a total of 1,070. Payday is the 5th and the balance on the 30th is usually 400. Without the calendar, that looks fine because each bill seems small. With the calendar, the 1st shows 1,070 against a 400 balance, so you can see a clear shortfall. You ask the phone and insurance providers to move their dates to the 8th, and the 1st drops to 970 and everything else sits after payday.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming autopay means safe. It only means the payment is attempted.
  • Ignoring variable bills. Put in a typical amount and review it when the real one arrives.
  • Forgetting annual and quarterly bills. Add them with the month they hit.
  • Keeping the list in your head. It stops working as soon as life gets busy.
  • Not updating after a change of bank, card or provider.
