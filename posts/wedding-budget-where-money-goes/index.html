How to Build a Wedding Budget That Survives Contact With Real Quotes

Most wedding budgets fall apart the same way: someone writes a total, books the venue, and then discovers that every other category is already over. The fix is not a bigger number. It is a budget that you update every time a real quote arrives.

Step 1: Set the ceiling first

Write down the most you can spend in total, including money from family if it is confirmed. Then subtract a buffer of around 10 percent for things nobody plans for, such as alterations, extra chairs, tips or a last-minute delivery fee. What is left is your working budget.

Step 2: Split it into categories by percentage

Start with rough shares, then adjust them to what matters to the two of you. A typical set of categories looks like this:

  • Venue and catering
  • Photography and video
  • Attire, rings and beauty
  • Flowers and decor
  • Music and entertainment
  • Stationery, postage and favours
  • Transport and accommodation
  • Officiant and legal fees
  • Buffer

Venue and catering usually take the biggest slice because they scale with guest count. If a category matters little to you, give it a small share on purpose rather than by accident.

Step 3: Three columns per line item

For every expense, track three numbers: Estimate (what you expect), Quote (what a supplier actually offered) and Paid (money gone). Add a Balance due column with =C2-D2 where C is the agreed cost and D is what you have paid so far, plus a Due date for the next payment.

Keeping estimate and quote apart is the trick. When a quote lands above the estimate, you see it immediately and can decide where to trim instead of finding out at the end.

Step 4: Make the guest count drive the numbers

Catering, stationery, favours and seating all depend on how many people come. Keep the guest list on its own sheet with these columns: name, household, side, invited (yes or no), RSVP status, meal choice, dietary notes and table number.

Then count confirmed guests with =COUNTIF(E:E,"Yes"), where column E is the RSVP status. Multiply by your per-head cost to see how a change in numbers moves the budget. Adding ten guests is never just ten chairs.

A worked example (invented numbers)

Suppose your total ceiling is 20,000, with a 10 percent buffer set aside.

  • Working budget: 20,000 - 2,000 = 18,000
  • Venue and catering at 45 percent: 8,100
  • Photography at 12 percent: 2,160

You get a catering quote of 85 per head. With 90 confirmed guests that is 7,650, which fits. If the guest list creeps to 110, it becomes 9,350, which is 1,250 over the allocation. Seeing that figure before you send more invitations lets you cut a few names, reduce the menu cost, or move money from decor, rather than quietly absorbing it.

Step 5: Review on a schedule

Set a monthly check. Compare total quotes against total budget, list payments due in the next 30 days and note anything still without a quote. In the last two months, move to weekly.

Common mistakes

  • No buffer. Small extras always appear. Treat the buffer as already spent.
  • Counting the plus-one that has not been confirmed. Only confirmed RSVPs belong in your catering numbers.
  • Forgetting tax and service charges. Ask whether quotes include them, and record the final figure.
  • Mixing gift money into the budget. Track it separately so it does not hide an overspend.
  • Not saving the contract dates. Deposit and final payment deadlines belong in the sheet, not just in an email.

Keep supplier names, phone numbers and contract notes beside each line so everything you need is in one place when something changes.
