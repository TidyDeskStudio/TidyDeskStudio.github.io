How to Do a Year-End Review of Your Small Business: Revenue, Best Clients and Next Year's Targets

When you work for yourself, the year ends without ceremony. Nobody hands you a report. Sales sit in invoices, costs sit in bank apps, and the clients you earned the most from are only a feeling. A one-hour review in December turns that fog into a few clear numbers and a plan.

Here is a method you can run in any spreadsheet.

Step 1: Total revenue and costs by month

Create twelve rows, January to December, with columns for revenue, costs and profit. Fill revenue from the date you were paid or invoiced, whichever you use consistently. Then:

=B2-C2

for profit each month, and a total row with =SUM(B2:B13). Add a margin column:

=IF(B2=0,0,D2/B2)

The pattern across the year is often more useful than the total. A quiet summer or a strong autumn tells you where to plan.

Step 2: Rank your clients

List every client or customer with the amount you earned from each. To put the biggest first, sort the column from largest to smallest. Then add a share column:

=B2/SUM($B$2:$B$20)

If one client is a large share of your year, that is a risk worth writing down, not a reason to panic. Equally, a client who takes lots of time for a small share deserves a conversation about price or scope.

Step 3: Run a year-end checklist

Use a short tick list so nothing is forgotten. Ideas to adapt to your own situation:

  • Send or chase any unpaid invoices.
  • Gather receipts and bank statements into one folder.
  • Record mileage and other regular costs while you remember them.
  • Note renewals due early next year, such as insurance or software.
  • Back up your files.

Which items matter depends on where you live and how you work, so adjust it.

Step 4: Look back with three questions

Write short answers, one or two sentences each:

  1. What brought in the most money for the least stress?
  2. What took far more time than it paid?
  3. What would I stop, start or change?

These answers are worth more than any chart.

Step 5: Set targets by quarter

Take this year's total and decide what you want next year. Then split it into four quarters so you can check in every three months instead of in December. Compare each quarter with its target:

=Actual-Target

A target set in numbers is easier to review than a wish.

A worked example

All figures are invented, for layout only.

  • Revenue for the year: 48,000. Costs: 15,000. Profit: 33,000, a margin of about 69%.
  • Clients: A brought 18,000 (37.5%), B 12,000 (25%), and six smaller clients 18,000 together.
  • The review shows that Client A is more than a third of income, and that the smaller clients take most of the admin time.
  • Next year's target of 54,000 becomes 13,500 per quarter, with a goal to add one new client at the A level.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing invoice date and payment date. Pick one and keep to it.
  • Ignoring costs. Revenue alone can look healthy while profit is thin.
  • Setting a target with no quarterly check. You find out in December again.
  • Leaving the review until the accountant asks. Do it while the year is fresh.

This is a record-keeping and planning method only, not tax, accounting or financial advice. Check your local rules or speak to a qualified professional.
