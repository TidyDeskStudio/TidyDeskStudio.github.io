Why Is My Utility Bill Higher? How to Tell If You Used More or Just Paid More

The heating comes on, the first cold-weather bill lands, and it's noticeably bigger than you expected. The obvious question is "did we use more, or did the price go up?" The bill rarely answers that clearly, and comparing it with last month doesn't help much, because October and August are not the same.

With a few columns and a year of bills, you can answer the question yourself in a couple of minutes. Here is how.

1. Compare with the same month last year, not last month

Energy use is seasonal. Electricity can climb in summer if you run cooling, gas climbs in winter, and water often rises in the garden months. Comparing this month to last month mostly shows you the season changing. The fair comparison is the same month last year, for the same utility.

So the first job is to gather old bills. Most providers let you download past statements from your online account. Twelve months is enough to start; two years is better.

2. Log every bill with usage, not just the amount

Make one Bills tab with a row per bill and these columns:

  • Utility - Electricity, Gas, Water, Heating oil
  • Bill date
  • Days covered - billing periods vary from 28 to 33 days or more, so this matters
  • Usage - the amount used, in kWh, m3, therms, litres or gallons, as printed on the bill
  • Amount - the total charged
  • Due date and Paid date

The usage figure is the key. Without it you can only see that a bill went up, not why.

3. Add three helper columns

With Days covered in C, Usage in D and Amount in E:

  • Cost per unit: =IF(D2=0,"",ROUND(E2/D2,3)). This is your effective price, including standing charges spread over what you used.
  • Cost per day: =IF(C2=0,"",ROUND(E2/C2,2)). This evens out short and long billing periods.
  • Same month last year: =SUMIFS(E:E,A:A,A2,B:B,">="&DATE(YEAR(B2)-1,MONTH(B2),1),B:B,"<"&DATE(YEAR(B2)-1,MONTH(B2)+1,1)). It finds last year's bill for the same utility and month.

Do the same for usage, then work out the change with =E2/J2-1 (this bill divided by last year's in column J, minus one).

4. Split the increase into usage and price

Now you can answer the real question. Compare two ratios:

  • Usage change = this year's usage / last year's usage - 1
  • Price change = this year's cost per unit / last year's cost per unit - 1

If usage is flat and the price is up, the rate changed and there's little you could have done at home. That's a reason to check your tariff or plan. If usage is up and the price is flat, something in the house changed: a new appliance, someone working from home, a colder month, a thermostat setting. If both went up, you'll see roughly how much of each.

A worked example

Example with invented numbers. Last October an electricity bill covered 30 days, 300 kWh, for 90. This October it covers 30 days, 340 kWh, for 112.

  • The bill is up 112 / 90 - 1 = about 24%.
  • Usage is up 340 / 300 - 1 = about 13%.
  • Cost per unit went from 0.300 to 0.329, up about 10%.

So roughly half the increase came from using more and half from a higher price. The household now knows to do two things: look at what changed at home this autumn, and check whether a better plan is available.

5. Use your own meter readings to catch estimates

Some bills are based on estimated readings. If you jot down the meter reading on the same day each month in a Readings tab (Date, Utility, Reading), you can work out usage per day between readings and compare it with the bill. A reading that's lower than the one before it is a typo or a meter problem, so flag it with something like =IF(C3<C2,"Check reading","OK").

Common mistakes

  • Comparing totals across different-length periods. A 33-day bill will look worse than a 28-day one. Use cost per day.
  • Ignoring the units. Gas might be in m3 on one bill and kWh on another. Keep one unit per utility.
  • Only keeping the amount. Without usage you can't tell price from consumption.
  • Reacting to one bill. One cold month can spike a bill. Look for a pattern over two or three bills before changing everything.
  • Forgetting the due date. A late fee wipes out any savings. Track the paid date too.

Ten minutes when the bill arrives

Enter the bill, glance at cost per unit and the change against last year, and you'll know straight away whether to look at your habits or your tariff.
