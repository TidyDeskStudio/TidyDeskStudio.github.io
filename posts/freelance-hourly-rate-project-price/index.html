How to Work Out Your Freelance Hourly Rate and Price a Project Without Guessing

You have a number in your head for what you want to earn, but when a client asks "what's your rate?" you either blurt out something low or freeze. The reason is usually simple: the number was never built from anything. Here is a way to build it from your own figures in about twenty minutes.

Step 1: Start with the income you need, not the rate you want

Write down what you need to take home in a year. Then add the things a job would normally cover for you: business expenses (software, equipment, insurance, a co-working desk if you use one), money set aside for taxes, and a buffer for slow months. The total is your target revenue, not your target pay.

This is a record-keeping exercise, not tax advice. How much to set aside for tax depends on where you live, so check local rules or ask an accountant.

Step 2: Count the hours you can really bill

Nobody bills 40 hours a week. Start with 52 weeks and subtract holidays, vacation, sick days and a few gap weeks. Then look at the working hours in each remaining week and subtract the time spent on emails, invoicing, marketing, quoting and learning. A realistic freelancer often bills somewhere around half to two thirds of the hours they work. Use your own history if you have it.

  • Weeks worked per year
  • Working hours per week
  • Share of those hours that are billable (as a percentage)

Billable hours per year = weeks x hours x billable share.

Step 3: Divide

Minimum hourly rate = target revenue / billable hours. In a spreadsheet, with revenue in B2 and billable hours in B3, that is =B2/B3. This is your floor. Anything below it means you are paying to work.

Worked example (invented numbers)

  • Take-home wanted: 48,000
  • Business expenses: 6,000
  • Tax set-aside and buffer: 14,000
  • Target revenue: 68,000
  • 46 working weeks x 30 hours x 60% billable = 828 billable hours
  • 68,000 / 828 = about 82 per hour

Notice that the rate is far higher than 48,000 / 2,080 hours (about 23). That gap is the whole reason freelancers undercharge: they divide by the hours of a job, not the hours they can invoice.

Step 4: Turn the hourly rate into a project price

Clients often prefer one number. To get it:

  1. Estimate the hours for each part of the work (briefing, drafts, revisions, final delivery).
  2. Add a contingency, commonly 15-25% for work with unclear scope.
  3. Multiply the total hours by your hourly rate.
  4. Add any hard costs (stock images, licences, subcontractors).

Example: 24 hours of work plus 20% contingency is 28.8 hours. At 82 per hour that is about 2,362, and you might quote 2,400. If the client later changes the brief, you can point to the hours you quoted and price the change the same way.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting unpaid time. Calls, revisions and chasing payment all take hours that never appear on an invoice. Include them in the estimate.
  • Copying a stranger's rate. Their costs and billable hours are not yours.
  • Never revisiting the number. Re-run it once or twice a year, and after any big change in expenses.
  • Quoting with no revision limit. Say how many rounds are included and what extra rounds cost.
  • Dropping the price when a client pushes back. Reduce the scope instead, so the hourly rate stays intact.

Keep the sums in one place

Put the inputs on one sheet, the formulas next to them, and change a single cell to see what a different expense total or billable share does to your rate.
