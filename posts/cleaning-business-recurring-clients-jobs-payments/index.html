How to Keep Track of Recurring Cleaning Clients, Jobs and Payments

A cleaning business runs on repeat customers: the Tuesday house, the every-other-Friday office, the monthly deep clean. That is great for steady income, and awkward for record keeping. Which clients are due this week? Did the Hendersons pay for the last two visits or just one? Did you quote the same price for the oven clean as last time? If the answers live in your head and a notes app, mistakes are coming.

Here is a setup that works in Excel or Google Sheets and takes an afternoon to build.

1. A client list with the details you need on the doorstep

One row per client. Columns that earn their place: client name, address, phone, access notes (alarm, key location, pets), service type, usual price, how often (weekly, fortnightly, monthly), preferred day, and a notes column for things like "skip the study" or "uses unscented products". Keep this tab tidy, because you will look at it more than any other.

2. A service price table

Instead of typing a price each time, keep a small table: standard clean, deep clean, oven, inside fridge, windows, move-out clean, plus an hourly rate for odd jobs. Name them plainly and change a price in one place when your costs rise.

3. A job log: one row per visit

This is the heart of it. Columns: date, client, service, hours worked, staff member, price charged, extras, paid (Yes/No), payment date, and notes. Calculate the price so it is never forgotten:

=VLOOKUP(C2,Prices!A:B,2,FALSE)+E2

Here C is the service name and E is any extras. VLOOKUP works the same in Excel and Google Sheets.

4. See who owes you

On a summary tab, list each client and total what they have not paid:

=SUMIFS(Jobs!F:F,Jobs!B:B,A2,Jobs!H:H,"No")

Sort the column largest first. Once a week, spend ten minutes sending a polite reminder to anyone with a balance. A visible number makes that job quick instead of dreaded.

5. Know what a job really earns

Revenue is not profit. Add two columns to the log: supplies cost (a rough per-visit figure is fine) and travel time or fuel. Then work out earnings per hour:

=(F2-G2)/D2

After a month, sort by that figure. You may find your cheapest-looking regular client is also your slowest, which is useful to know before you set next year's prices.

6. A weekly view

Add a tab that lists the next seven days of jobs. In a spreadsheet you can do this with a date filter on the job log, or a simple table with the days across the top and a row per cleaner. The aim is that on Sunday evening you can see the week at a glance and spot double bookings.

Worked example

Invented names and numbers.

  • Client: Marlowe Street flat, fortnightly standard clean at 70, plus an oven clean at 25 this visit.
  • Hours worked: 3. Supplies: 4.
  • Charged: 70 + 25 = 95. Earnings per hour: (95 - 4) / 3 = about 30.
  • Client paid 70 on the day, so 25 is outstanding and shows up on the summary tab.

Common mistakes

  • Not writing down extras. The oven, the fridge and the extra hour are where money quietly disappears.
  • Quoting from memory. A price table keeps quotes consistent between clients.
  • Letting small balances pile up. Two unpaid visits feel awkward to raise; one does not.
  • No record of access details. Keep them private and accurate, and update them when a client tells you something changed.
  • Ignoring cancellations. Add a status column (done, cancelled, rescheduled) so your numbers match reality.

This is a record-keeping method, not tax or legal advice; check your local rules for insurance, licensing and reporting income.
