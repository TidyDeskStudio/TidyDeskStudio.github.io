How to Plan a Week of Meals and Keep Grocery Spending Under Budget

Food spending creeps. Dinner gets decided at 5pm, a few extras land in the trolley, and by the end of the month the grocery total is higher than you meant, with nothing obvious to blame.

Meal planning fixes this by moving decisions from the stressful moment to a calm one. You don't need a rigid menu, just a short weekly routine.

Step 1: Check what you already have

Before planning, spend five minutes looking in the fridge, freezer and cupboards. Write down anything that needs using soon. Building meals around it is the easiest saving there is.

Step 2: Plan dinners first

Make a table with the days of the week down the side and a column for each meal you care about. Most people only need dinner at first. Give yourself a mix:

  • Two or three meals you cook from scratch.
  • One that uses leftovers or freezer stock.
  • One quick, low-effort night for the busiest day.
  • One flexible night for plans that change.

Repeating favourites is fine. The goal is fewer last-minute decisions, not variety for its own sake.

Step 3: Build one shopping list from the plan

Go meal by meal and write each ingredient you don't already have. Group the list by shop section (produce, dairy, tins, frozen) so you walk the aisles once. Give each line a quantity, an estimated price and a category. Tick off items as you buy them.

Step 4: Price the list before you leave home

Add the estimates up:

=SUM(D2:D40)

Compare the total with your weekly food budget:

=B1-SUM(D2:D40)

If the answer is negative, you can still change the plan. Swap one pricey meal for a cheaper one and watch the total drop. Doing this at the kitchen table is far easier than doing it at the till.

Step 5: Record what you actually spent

After shopping, enter the real receipt total in a weekly log with four columns: week starting, budget, actual, difference. Difference is simply:

=C2-B2

A positive number means over budget. Over a month you can add the weekly rows to see the total and spot patterns, such as one week each month that always runs over because of a top-up shop.

A worked example

All numbers invented, for layout only.

  • Weekly food budget: 95.
  • Planned list total: 88. Under budget by 7 before leaving home.
  • Receipt: 94, because two unplanned snacks and a drink were added.
  • Four-week totals: 95, 95, 95, 95 budgeted (380) against 94, 103, 90, 99 actual (386). Six over for the month, and week two is the one to look at.

You don't need to be perfect. You just need to see which week and which habit moved the number.

Common mistakes

  • Planning seven elaborate dinners. Start with five and leave two flexible.
  • Shopping hungry or without a list. Both lead to extras.
  • Ignoring the freezer. Forgotten food is wasted money.
  • Never recording the actual spend. Without it, the budget is only a wish.
  • Setting a budget too tight to keep. Start from your real recent spending, then trim gradually.

This is a household planning and record-keeping method, not diet, nutrition or financial advice. If you have dietary needs, plan around them first.
