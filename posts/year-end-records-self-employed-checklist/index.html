It's the week you finally open the folder called "Business 2026" and find three receipts, a screenshot and a bank statement from March. Year-end paperwork is miserable mostly because it's treated as one huge job. Split it into five small piles and it stops being scary.

This is a record-keeping method only. It isn't tax or legal advice, and the rules differ by country, so check what applies to you or ask a qualified person.

Pile 1: Income

List every payment you received in the year: invoices paid, cash, marketplace payouts, tips. Add a column for the date received, the client, the amount and the invoice number.

Then check two things. First, does your total match what actually landed in your bank accounts and payment apps? Second, are there invoices you sent in December that are still unpaid? Note them separately so you know what belongs to which period under your local rules.

Pile 2: Expenses

Go through bank and card statements month by month and tag each business spend with a category. A short list works better than a long one: software, supplies, equipment, travel, phone and internet, insurance, professional fees, advertising, other.

For each one, record the date, who you paid, the amount and whether you have the receipt. A Receipt? column with yes or no tells you instantly what's missing. To total a category in Excel or Google Sheets:

=SUMIF(C:C,"Software",E:E)

where column C holds the category and column E the amount.

Pile 3: Mileage and travel

If you drive for work, you need a log with the date, start and end point, purpose and distance. Reconstructing a year of trips from memory is rough, so use your calendar and sent invoices as prompts. Total the distance with =SUM(F:F).

Pile 4: Assets and big purchases

Laptop, camera, tools, furniture. List anything expensive you bought for the business, with the date, price and where the receipt is. Your accountant will want to know the lot, and you'll want a record when the item is old.

Pile 5: Documents

Make a simple checklist of paperwork to keep with the year's records: bank and card statements, payout reports, loan statements, insurance documents, any forms you received from clients or platforms, and last year's filed return for reference. Tick each one as you save a copy to your records folder.

A worked example (invented numbers)

Jordan Example is a freelance photographer. Their quick check looks like this:

  • Income listed from invoices and payouts: 41,200
  • Money received in bank and payment apps: 41,200 (matches)
  • Unpaid December invoices: 1,500, noted separately
  • Expenses with a receipt: 14 of 16 lines; two missing
  • Mileage logged: 2,340 miles

The two missing receipts are the only real task left. Jordan emails the suppliers for copies, and the folder is done in an afternoon instead of a week.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with receipts. Start with bank statements; they show everything you actually spent, and you match receipts to them.
  • Mixing personal and business spending. If an item is partly personal, write a note about how you decided the split.
  • Throwing away the supporting paper too soon. Check your local rules for how long records must be kept.
  • Leaving it until January. Do a quick monthly tidy, even 15 minutes, and year end becomes a review.

Make next year easier

Set a repeating reminder for the last Friday of each month: add income, add expenses, tick off receipts. The same five piles stay up to date, and by December you're only checking, not hunting.
