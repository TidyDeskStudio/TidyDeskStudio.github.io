How to Keep a Car Maintenance Log and Work Out Your Real Fuel Economy

You know the feeling: the mechanic asks when the last oil change was, and you shrug. Or a warning light comes on and you cannot remember if the brake pads were done last year or the year before. A maintenance log fixes that, and it takes about two minutes a month once it is set up.

Start with one row per event

The easiest log is a single table where every service, repair or fill-up is one row. Use these columns:

  • Date
  • Odometer (miles or km at the time)
  • Type (oil change, tyres, brakes, repair, inspection, fuel)
  • Description (what was actually done)
  • Cost
  • Where (shop name or DIY)
  • Next due odometer and next due date

Always record the odometer, not just the date. Most service intervals are set by distance, so the odometer is what tells you when something is due.

Add a "next due" column and let it nag you

When you log an oil change at 42,000 miles and your manual says every 5,000, put 47,000 in the next-due column. Then add one cell near the top with your current odometer and a status formula beside each due item:

=IF($B$1>=G5,"DUE",IF($B$1>=G5-500,"Soon","OK"))

Here B1 holds today's odometer and G5 is that row's next-due reading. It works the same in Excel and Google Sheets. Check your owner's manual for the actual intervals; they differ a lot between vehicles.

Track fuel separately

Fill-ups work best on their own sheet with columns for date, odometer, litres or gallons added, total cost and whether you filled the tank completely. That last column matters, because fuel economy is only accurate between two full tanks.

The calculation is simple. Distance since the last full fill, divided by the fuel added at this full fill:

=(B3-B2)/C3

B is the odometer, C is the fuel added. For cost per mile, use =D3/(B3-B2). Do not judge a single tank; look at the average of five or six, because weather, traffic and a heavy load can swing one fill a long way.

A worked example (invented numbers)

Say your last full fill was at 30,000 miles. At 30,320 miles you fill up again and the pump takes 10.0 gallons for $38.00.

  • Distance: 30,320 - 30,000 = 320 miles
  • Economy: 320 / 10.0 = 32 miles per gallon
  • Cost per mile: $38.00 / 320 = about $0.12

If next month the same trip gives 27 mpg, you have a reason to check tyre pressure or an air filter before it turns into a bigger problem. Without the log you would just feel that the car seemed thirstier.

What else is worth recording

  • Tyre rotations and replacements. Note the date and odometer so you can see how many miles a set really lasted.
  • Warranty and recall dates. Put the expiry in a note so it does not slip by.
  • Parts you bought. Brand and part number save a lot of time at the counter next time.
  • Small stuff. Wiper blades, bulbs and fluids add up over a year.

Keep the receipts as photos in a folder with the same date as the log row. If you ever sell the car, a dated list of services is a strong selling point for a buyer.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping the odometer. Dates alone cannot tell you when the next service is due.
  • Counting partial fills. If you did not fill to the same point, leave that row out of the economy average.
  • Only logging big jobs. The cheap routine items are the ones people forget, and they prevent the expensive ones.
  • Mixing vehicles in one table. Give each vehicle its own sheet, or add a vehicle column and filter.
  • Using the log for work mileage. A maintenance log is not a trip log; keep business trips in a separate record.

Make it a habit

Log fuel when you fill up, using your phone to jot the numbers in a notes app if the spreadsheet is not handy, then copy them across at the weekend. Set a reminder on the first of each month to look at the status column. That is the whole routine.
