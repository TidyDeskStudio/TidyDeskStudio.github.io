How to Review Your Freelance Clients at Year-End and Decide Who Gets a Rate Raise

Most freelancers raise their rates the same way: they feel nervous, put it off, and then quietly keep the old price for another year. Meanwhile some clients take far more time than they pay for, and others are a joy at a price you could easily lift.

A short year-end review gives you facts to work from, so a rate rise is a plan and not a gamble. Here is a method you can do in an afternoon.

Step 1: List every client from the year

One row per client. If you invoiced them even once, they go in. Pull the data from your invoices, not from memory.

Step 2: Add the numbers that matter

  • Total invoiced this year.
  • Hours worked - your best estimate, or from a timer if you used one. Include emails, calls and revisions.
  • Effective hourly rate - invoiced divided by hours.
  • Current rate - what you quote them.
  • Average days to pay - invoice date to money in.
  • Scope creep - Low, Medium or High.
  • Enjoyment - a score from 1 to 5.
  • Last rate change - the date.

Effective hourly rate in column F, with invoiced in C and hours in D:

=IF(D2=0,"",C2/D2)

Share of total income for each client, which shows how dependent you are on any one of them:

=C2/SUM($C$2:$C$30)

Step 3: Sort three ways

Sort by effective hourly rate, lowest first. The bottom of that list is where you are being paid least per hour. Then sort by share of income to spot any client who is a large part of your total. Finally sort by days to pay, since a client who pays late costs you in stress and cash flow, even if their rate looks fine.

Step 4: Give each client a decision

Add a column called Plan and choose one of these for every row:

  • Raise - increase the rate from a set date.
  • Hold - keep as is, revisit next year.
  • Reprice scope - same rate, but tighter terms on revisions or extras.
  • Phase out - stop taking new work from them.

A worked example

All of this is invented, only to show how it plays out.

  • Client A: invoiced 6,000, 100 hours, effective rate 60. Pays in 12 days. Enjoyment 5.
  • Client B: invoiced 4,500, 150 hours, effective rate 30. Pays in 45 days. Scope creep High.
  • Client C: invoiced 2,000, 25 hours, effective rate 80. Pays in 7 days.

Client B is working hard for the least. The plan here might be Reprice scope: set a revision limit and a higher rate for extra rounds. Client A is doing well but is a large share of the total, so a modest raise plus an effort to find another client of the same kind makes sense. Client C is already strong, so Hold.

Step 5: Choose the increase and the date

Decide the new rate before you write the message. Pick a start date that is far enough away to be fair, for example the start of next quarter, and keep any current project at the old rate unless you agreed otherwise. Write the number into a New rate column so you can compare it to the current one:

=(H2-G2)/G2

Put the percentage change in its own column. If the figure surprises you, it helps to see it before you send anything.

Step 6: Write a short, plain note

Keep it brief: thank them, state the new rate, give the date it starts, and offer to talk if they have questions. Skip long justifications. Log the date you sent it and their reply in the sheet.

Common mistakes

  • Judging by invoice size alone. A big client with endless revisions can pay less per hour than a small one.
  • Forgetting unbilled time. Calls and messages count.
  • Springing a rise on a live project. Give notice and start on a new job or quarter.
  • Not recording the outcome. Next year you will want to know who accepted and when.
  • Waiting for a perfect moment. There isn't one. A set review date is better.

This is a record-keeping and planning tool, not financial or tax advice. Check local rules for anything that affects your business.
