How to Price Handmade Products So Your Time Actually Gets Paid

Most makers set a price by looking at what similar items sell for and going a little lower. It feels safe, but it skips the only question that matters: after materials, fees and the hours you spent, is there anything left? With craft fair and holiday orders coming up, it's worth an evening to get this right before you print price tags.

Here's a pricing method you can set up in a spreadsheet and reuse for every product you make.

1. Work out what one unit of each material costs

You buy supplies in packs: a 5 kg bag of wax, a 250 ml bottle of fragrance, a box of 100 wicks. Your product uses a slice of that pack, so start with a Materials tab:

  • Material and Supplier
  • Pack price - what you paid, including delivery if you can
  • Pack quantity and Unit - 5000 and g, 250 and ml, 100 and pcs
  • Cost per unit - =IF(OR(C2="",D2=0),"",ROUND(C2/D2,4))

Keep four decimal places. A wax cost of 0.0064 per gram looks like nothing until you multiply it by 400 grams.

2. Write a recipe for each product

Next, list what goes into each item, one row per material: product name, material, quantity used. A lookup pulls in the cost per unit from the Materials tab:

=IFERROR(INDEX(Materials!F:F,MATCH(B2,Materials!A:A,0)),0)

Multiply by the quantity and you have a line cost. Add up the line costs for each product with SUMIFS, and your material cost updates by itself when a supplier raises prices. You change one pack price, not twenty products.

3. Pay yourself for the making time

This is where most handmade prices fall apart. Time a real batch, divide by the number of items, and record the minutes per item. Then:

Labour = Minutes to make / 60 * Your hourly rate

Pick an hourly rate you'd be happy to earn, not the lowest one you'd tolerate. If labour makes the final price look too high, that's useful to know: it tells you to speed up the process, change the product, or find buyers who value it.

4. Add packaging, overhead and fees

Packaging (boxes, labels, tissue) goes in per item. Overhead covers things that aren't in any one product: tools, electricity, workspace, the test pieces you threw away. A simple way is a percentage on top of your direct costs, for example 10%.

Selling fees are different because they're taken from the selling price, not added to your cost. Marketplace and card fees together often take a noticeable slice, so check your own platform's fee page and use that percentage.

5. Turn cost into wholesale and retail prices

A common starting point among makers is:

  • Total cost = materials + labour + packaging + other costs + overhead
  • Wholesale price = total cost x 2
  • Retail price = wholesale price x 2

Even if you never sell to shops, set the retail price as if you might. If a shop asks for wholesale later, you can say yes without losing money. Then check the result: Profit = Price - Price * Fee % - Total cost and Margin = Profit / Price.

A worked example (invented numbers)

Take a 200 g soy candle. Wax at 0.0064 per gram comes to 1.28, fragrance 1.15, a wick 0.06 and the jar 1.00, so materials are 3.49. It takes 12 minutes, and at an hourly rate of 20 that's 4.00 of labour. Packaging is 0.60. That's 8.09, plus 10% overhead, for a total cost of 8.90.

Doubled twice, that gives a wholesale price of 17.80 and a retail price of 35.60. After a 10% fee, profit at retail is 23.14. If you'd rather sell at 22, the fee is 2.20 and profit is still 10.90, on top of the labour you've already paid yourself. The lowest price that covers costs and fees is 8.90 / (1 - 10%), about 9.89. Anything under that loses money on every sale.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Leaving out your time. If labour isn't in the cost, the profit you see is really your wage, and it's usually low.
  • Forgetting fees come off the top. Ten percent of a 30 price is 3, every single sale.
  • Pricing from one pack price forever. Update the Materials tab whenever you restock.
  • Matching the cheapest seller. You don't know their costs, or whether they're making anything.
  • One price for every channel. Markets, your own site and wholesale have different costs, so check the margin for each.
