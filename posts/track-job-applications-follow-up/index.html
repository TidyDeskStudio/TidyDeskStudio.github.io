How to Track Job Applications and Never Miss a Follow-Up

A job search turns into a pile of tabs, emails and half-remembered conversations very quickly. By the third week you are asking yourself whether you already applied to that company, who the recruiter was, and whether it is too late to follow up. A simple tracking sheet answers all of that in seconds.

Decide what to record

Keep it small enough that you will actually use it. One row per application, with these columns:

  • Company and Job title
  • Link to the posting (postings get taken down, so also paste the key requirements into a notes column)
  • Date applied
  • Status (Saved, Applied, Screening, Interview, Offer, Rejected, Withdrawn)
  • Contact name and email
  • Next step and Next step date
  • Salary range if listed
  • Notes

Use a status list, not free text

In Excel or Google Sheets, add a drop-down to the Status column using data validation with the list above. It keeps the entries consistent so you can count and filter them. Count how many are at each stage with:

=COUNTIF(D:D,"Interview")

where D is the status column. A small summary at the top showing how many are applied, in progress and closed gives you a quick read on how the search is going.

The follow-up column is the important one

Most people track what they applied for but not what they must do next. Add a Next step date and highlight overdue rows with conditional formatting. In Google Sheets, apply a custom formula rule to the range: =AND($G2<>"",$G2<=TODAY()), where G holds the next step date. Rows turn a colour when it is time to act.

Sensible default timings you can adapt: a short follow-up note about a week after applying if the posting gave no deadline, and a thank-you email within a day after any interview. If an employer gave you a date for a decision, use that date instead.

A worked example (invented data)

Imagine four rows after one week:

  • Harbour Supplies Ltd, Operations Assistant, applied 3 Nov, status Applied, next step: follow-up on 10 Nov
  • Northfield Clinic, Office Coordinator, applied 4 Nov, status Screening, next step: phone call Thursday
  • Brightline Print Co, Customer Support, applied 5 Nov, status Interview, next step: send thank-you note
  • Oakmere Logistics, Dispatcher, applied 5 Nov, status Rejected

A glance shows one follow-up due, one call to prepare for and one thank-you to send. The rejected row stays in the sheet, because if the same company posts a new role later you will remember how it went last time.

Track the results of the process, too

After a month, count how many applications led to a reply and how many to an interview. If you are applying widely and getting few replies, the problem may be your CV or how well you match the role. If you are getting interviews but no offers, look at your preparation. Use your own numbers to decide where to spend effort, not guesses.

Add an optional column for Where you found it. After a few weeks you will see which sources produce replies and which ones waste your time.

Common mistakes

  • Not saving the job description. You will want it before the interview, and by then it may be gone.
  • Tracking only the applications you sent. Include roles you saved but have not applied for, with a deadline.
  • Letting the sheet go stale. Update it the same day something changes.
  • Deleting rejections. They hold useful history.
  • Using a different CV version each time without noting it. Add a column for which version you sent.
