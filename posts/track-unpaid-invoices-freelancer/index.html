How to Track Unpaid Invoices as a Freelancer (and Chase Them Before It Gets Awkward)

Most freelancers don't lose money because clients refuse to pay. They lose it because an invoice slips out of sight. You send it, start the next project, and six weeks later you realise nobody ever paid for that logo refresh. By then the follow-up email feels awkward, so it waits another week.

A simple invoice log stops that from happening. It takes a few minutes to set up in Excel or Google Sheets and about two minutes a week to keep current.

1. One row per invoice, not per client

It's tempting to keep one row per client with a running total. Don't. The moment a client has two open invoices, or pays one and not the other, the totals stop making sense. Give every invoice its own row with these columns:

  • Invoice # - a simple running number like 2026-031
  • Client and Service
  • Invoice date and Terms (days) - for example 14 or 30
  • Amount
  • Amount paid and Date paid

Add the row the same day you send the invoice. If it isn't in the log, it doesn't exist.

2. Let the sheet work out the due date

Typing due dates by hand is where mistakes creep in. If Invoice date is in column D and Terms in column E, the due date is:

=IF(D2="","",D2+E2)

If most clients are on the same terms, put your default (say 14) in a single cell on a settings tab and use it whenever the Terms column is blank.

3. Add a balance and a status column

With Amount in G and Amount paid in H, the balance is:

=IF(G2="","",G2-SUM(H2))

SUM(H2) treats a blank as zero, so new invoices don't show errors. Then a status column turns the numbers into something you can scan, with Due date in F and Balance in I:

=IF(G2="","",IF(I2<=0,"Paid",IF(TODAY()>F2,"Overdue",IF(F2-TODAY()<=3,"Due soon","Open"))))

Use conditional formatting to colour Overdue red and Due soon amber. Now one glance tells you who to contact.

4. Count the days late

Knowing an invoice is overdue is useful. Knowing it's 3 days overdue versus 33 tells you what kind of message to send:

=IF(AND(I2>0,TODAY()>F2),TODAY()-F2,"")

A few days late usually needs a friendly nudge. A month late needs a firmer email and maybe a pause on new work.

5. Set a follow-up rhythm

Decide your reminder steps once, so you're not agonising over each one:

  1. Due date: a short, friendly "just a reminder this is due today" with the invoice attached again.
  2. 7 days late: a polite check-in asking if they need anything from you to process it.
  3. 21 days late: a direct note with the balance, the original due date, and when you'll pause further work.

Add a Last reminder date column so you know where each invoice is in that sequence.

A quick example

Here's an invented example. You send three invoices in September: 600 to Client A on 30-day terms, 1,200 to Client B on 14-day terms, and 450 to Client C on 14-day terms. By 14 October, Client A has paid in full. Client B paid 700 and still owes 500, 16 days late. Client C hasn't paid and is 9 days late. Your log shows 950 outstanding across two invoices, which means one firmer email and one polite check-in this morning.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Marking part payments as paid. Record exactly what arrived and let the balance show the rest.
  • Spelling client names differently. Use a dropdown from a client list so totals per client add up.
  • Deleting paid rows. Keep them. A year of history shows which clients always pay late and what you earned.
  • Only checking when money feels tight. Pick one day a week, like Monday morning, and look at the Overdue rows.

This is about keeping clear records. It isn't tax or legal advice, so check the rules where you live.
